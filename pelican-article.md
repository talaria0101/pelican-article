## Chapter 1. The Bowl-Billed Boundary Crossers

Stand on any warm coast between the tropics and the temperate zone and wait. If pelicans live within a hundred kilometers, the odds are good that one will eventually cross the sky overhead in a slow, unhurried glide, wings bent like an architect's first sketch of flight. The bird is too big to be elegant and too graceful to be clumsy, and it carries in front of it the most famous piece of anatomy in the bird world: a bill with a pouch, a structure that looks improvised and works like a seine net.

A pelican is easy to recognize and surprisingly hard to define. Every bird-watcher knows one on sight, yet ask a dozen people what the pouch is actually for and you will get a dozen wrong answers, most of them borrowed from cartoons. The pelican does not ferry fish around in its pouch for later the way a kangaroo ferries a joey. It does not scoop up ocean water and drink the ocean. The pouch is a tool, not a purse, and much of this article is about what a good tool it turns out to be.

Eight species of pelican are alive today. They breed on every continent except Antarctica and South America south of Peru, though individual birds wander far beyond those limits. Two of them, the brown pelican and the Peruvian pelican, hunt by plunging out of the air into the sea like feathered darts. The other six fish on the water's surface, often in groups that move like a well-drilled crew hauling nets. Between those two styles lies one shared design: a huge body, long wings built for soaring, short legs with fully webbed feet, and that great expandable throat.

The pelican occupies an odd position in the human imagination. On one side, it is sacred history: the medieval emblem of a mother bird feeding her young from her own body, carved into churches, painted into royal portraits, and stamped onto the great seal of the state of Louisiana. On the other side, it is internet comedy: a bird that swallows a pigeon whole in a London park while tourists film in disbelief, or that loafs on a dock pilfering fish from fishermen with the calm of a landlord collecting rent. Few animals have been so loved and so slandered in the same breath. The limerick that everyone half remembers, the one about the bird whose bill holds more than his belican, was written in 1910 and has been quoted ever since, usually wrong, usually with affection.

This mixture of reverence and ridicule has a long history and it says as much about people as about pelicans. Humans fish, so humans have always resented, studied, envied, and copied a bird that fishes better than they do on its own best days. The word for the bird entered the Bible as an emblem of desolation and entered the heraldry of Europe as an emblem of self-sacrifice. A Florida island full of nesting pelicans became the first federal wildlife refuge in the United States. A village in southern India shares its trees with a pelican colony and considers the birds lucky. A prison in northern California is named for the bay where they once gathered by the thousand.

The plan of this article is straightforward. Chapters 2 through 4 cover the bird itself: where it sits on the tree of life, how the pouch works, and how the rest of the body is built around that strange centerpiece. Chapters 5 through 13 profile the eight living species one by one, from the giant Dalmatian pelican of European wetlands to the small pink-backed birds that nest in African trees. Chapters 14 through 17 deal with behavior: how pelicans feed, how they breed, and how they move across continents. Chapters 18 through 20 follow the long, tangled story of pelicans and people, including the conservation victories and the current threats. Chapters 21 through 24 close with open scientific questions, practical advice for anyone who wants to watch pelicans responsibly, a set of frequently asked questions, and a conclusion.

A warning about numbers is worth giving early. Pelicans are large, visible birds, and this tempts everyone, including scientists, into confident estimates. Population figures for pelicans swing wildly with fish stocks, flood cycles, and disease outbreaks. Where exact numbers are uncertain, this article says so, and prefers honest ranges to false precision. Pelicans have been miscounted, misclassified, and misquoted for three thousand years. This article tries not to add to the pile.

One thing can be promised. By the last page, the reader will know what the pouch actually does, why a brown pelican can survive a headfirst crash into the sea from six stories up, why a medieval queen was painted as a pelican, why the second chick in many pelican nests almost never survives, and why the bird that looks like a joke is one of the most successful designs in the history of seabirds. The pelican has outlived empires, graced the oldest wildlife sanctuaries on earth, and rebuilt populations that people once wrote off as finished. It deserves a long article. Here it is.
## Chapter 2. Naming the Pelican: Etymology and a Family Tree

Every scientific name is a small piece of frozen history, and the pelican carries one of the oldest. Carl Linnaeus himself described the great white pelican in the tenth edition of his Systema Naturae in 1758 and gave the genus its name, Pelecanus, borrowing a word the Greeks had used for the bird for centuries. The Greek pelekan may descend from pelekys, the word for an axe, a nod to that heavy bill, though ancient writers already disagreed about the derivation. The scientific name of the great white pelican, onocrotalus, is a loan from an even older Greek word whose literal meaning has been argued over since antiquity. Whatever it originally meant, the name has been attached to the bird for longer than most languages have had a written form. In English the word arrived through Old French and Latin and was being written down by the fourteenth century.

Taxonomically, the pelicans form the family Pelecanidae, a family with exactly one living genus. That is a strangely tidy arrangement. There are no living pelican relatives that are not pelicans. The family sits inside the order Pelecaniformes, and here the story gets interesting, because the contents of that order have been reshuffled repeatedly over the last two centuries. Older textbooks stuffed herons, storks, ibises, spoonbills, and flamingos in with the pelicans on the basis of overall similarity. Mid twentieth century revisions moved things out. Then molecular genetics moved almost everything back.

The modern consensus, built on DNA comparisons from the 1990s onward, is that Pelecaniformes includes the pelicans, the herons, the ibises and spoonbills, and, most surprisingly, two of the strangest birds in the world: the shoebill of central African swamps and the hamerkop of Africa and Arabia. Genetic work published in the 2000s put the shoebill and the hamerkop close to the pelican line, with the shoebill and hamerkop often recovered as sisters to each other or to the pelicans themselves. Think about that for a moment. The nearest living kin of the pelican may be a giant stork-shaped swamp predator with a shoe for a face, and a small brown bird that builds the largest roofed nests of any bird on earth. Convergent evolution works in mysterious directions. The old textbook neighbors of the pelicans, the cormorants, boobies, gannets, frigatebirds, and darters, are now placed in the separate order Suliformes, while the tropicbirds sit alone in Phaethontiformes.

The logic of the pelican body plan nevertheless rhymes with that of the gannets and boobies, and for a simple reason: some pelicans and some boobies hunt in the same way, striking prey from the air and diving after it. Similar jobs shape similar bodies. But the pelican arrived at the plunge-dive from its own direction, keeping a bill the length of a man's forearm, while the gannets evolved spear-like bills and strike like a thrown javelin. Two engineering schools, one contract.

How long has the design existed? The fossil record for pelicans is patchy but respectable by seabird standards. Bones clearly attributable to the genus Pelecanus appear in European deposits roughly 30 million years old, and fragmentary older material from the Eocene may belong to the same lineage. For perspective, 30 million years places pelicans before the existence of most living groups of mammals, well before any ape that would recognize itself in a mirror. Pelicans flew over the Oligocene seas. They watched the Mediterranean dry up and refill, they circled over the grasslands that would become the Sahara, and they are still here, nearly unchanged in outline, while dynasties of mammals rose and fell around their nesting cliffs.

Within the single living genus, systematists recognize eight species. The relationships among the eight are mostly unresolved in detail, but a few things seem secure. The brown pelican of the Americas and the Peruvian pelican of the Humboldt Current are sister species, so recently separated that older authorities treated them as one. The great white pelican of the Old World and the American white pelican are an Old World and New World pair, close in appearance and behavior. The Dalmatian pelican and the spot-billed pelican are the Old World's other pairing, and the pink-backed pelican and Australian pelican round out the family. Confusingly, the two "white" pelicans are not each other's closest relatives in some analyses, a reminder that gene trees and appearance trees do not always agree.

The eight species fall neatly into two feeding guilds, and this split organizes much of the rest of this article. The plunge-divers are the brown and Peruvian pelicans, marine birds of the American Pacific and Atlantic coasts that feed alone or in small groups and fish by crashing into the sea from the air. The group-fishers are the six remaining species, birds of inland waters and sheltered coasts that feed while swimming, often cooperatively, herding fish into shallow water or bays before scooping them up. The two guilds differ in habitat, in social behavior, in breeding biology, and in the problems they face from people. They share the pouch, the wingspan, the colonial habit, and a talent for making themselves at home wherever fish are careless.

A final word on classification as a mirror of human fashion. Eight species is the current count, but older books say seven, because the Peruvian pelican was long merged into the brown. Older still authorities spoke casually of a dozen forms. The count will probably hold at eight for now, but pelican taxonomy, like pelican behavior, rewards patience in anyone who waits by the water.
## Chapter 3. The Pouch: Anatomy of a Living Net

Everything people think they know about pelicans concentrates in the pouch, and almost all of it is wrong in instructive ways. So begin with demolition. The pouch is not a storage locker where a pelican keeps fish for later. It is not a water bottle. It does not expand because it is loose or saggy; it is a precise, muscular, vascularized organ under active control at all times. And a pelican cannot "scoop up" a swimmer, a dog, or, despite viral video captions, a toddler. The muscles that close the bill are simply not strong enough, and the pouch is not shaped to hold them. What the pouch actually is, is one of the most elegant fishing tools evolution has produced, and understanding it takes only a few paragraphs.

Start with the skeleton, because the pouch begins there. A pelican's lower bill is not a single rigid shovel like a duck's. The two thin bony halves, the rami, are hinged to the skull in a way that lets them bow outward and flex independently. When a pelican plunges or scoops, the rami spread apart like the opening of a net, and the floor of the mouth, a sheet of elastic skin stretching between the two branches of the lower jaw and anchored up toward the hyoid apparatus under the tongue, drops downward to form the bag. The skin itself is bare of feathers, thin, and richly supplied with blood vessels. In a large pelican the pouch can hold on the order of eleven liters, roughly three times the volume of the bird's stomach. The old limerick was closer to the truth than its author could have hoped: the bill really does hold more than the belly can.

The fishing stroke is fast and brutal. In the plunge-divers, the bird hits the water folded like a closed knife, opens the bill underwater, and everts the pouch forward so that fish and water are swept into it in a fraction of a second. In the group-fishers, the bird swimming at the surface dips its open bill underwater and thrusts the pouch forward through a fish school, a motion scullers would recognize as a scooping catch. Either way the bird comes up with a pouch hanging heavy and full of water, often several liters of water carrying a few hundred grams of fish. Then comes the part nobody puts in the cartoons: draining. The pelican tilts its head, contracts the pouch, and forces the water out through the corners of the mouth, holding the fish back with its tongue and the pouch itself, the way a net man brings the bunt of a seine up out of the water. Only then does it throw its head back and swallow. A brown pelican can complete the whole sequence, dive to swallow, in a few seconds.

Why carry water out with the fish? Because water is the working medium of the net. A net catches fish by displacing water, and the pouch is the same trick run in reverse: the bird displaces water with a bag, the fish come along, and the water is poured back out. The pouch's size is sized to the water displacement needed to surround a lunging strike, not to the bird's appetite.

The pouch has a second job that most people never see, because heat is invisible. The bare, thin, well-blooded skin of the pouch and bill is a radiator. Pelicans work hard, loaf in tropical sun, and have no sweat glands. On a hot day a pelican sits with its mouth slightly open, quivering the throat muscles in a rapid flutter called gular flutter, pumping air across the wet inner surface of the pouch and cooling blood in the vessels just under the skin. Thermal-imaging studies of pelicans and of other birds with outsized bills, toucans are the famous comparison, show the bill and pouch radiating heat far above body temperature into the surrounding air. On cool mornings the same surface can be clamped shut to conserve heat. The pouch is thus a thermostat with a fish net attached, and the bird switches its function as casually as a person changes a fan from exhaust to intake.

The pouch is also a billboard. In most species its color changes with breeding condition, driven by hormones. Great white pelicans develop a pink wash with yellow and blue accents on the pouch at the height of the season. Australian pelican pouches blush pink to red. Brown pelican pouches darken toward a deep, dull red in display. Dalmatian pelican pouches flare orange-red. Pairs of pelicans court face to face, bills crossed and pouches presented, and the color does some of the talking.

Finally, the pouch is fragile in ways that bring it into constant contact with human affairs. Fish hooks tear it. Monofilament line wraps around it and cuts it as the bird swims. Discarded plastic and fishing lures lodge in it. Rehabilitators who work on pelicans spend a large share of their repair time on pouch wounds, and the news is good: pouch skin heals remarkably well when cleaned and sutured, and successfully repaired pelicans are routinely released back to the wild. The organ that makes the pelican famous is also the organ most likely to land it in a rescue center, which is worth remembering by anyone who fishes near where pelicans feed.
## Chapter 4. Built for Water and Wind: Skeleton, Wings, and Feathers

The pouch is the pelican's most visible organ but it is only the handle of the tool. The rest of the bird is an engineering package for a specific problem: a big, heavy, fish-eating flying animal that must live on the water, on the wing, and on crowded breeding islands, and must do all three cheaply enough to survive lean seasons.

Begin with weight, because everything follows from it. Pelicans range from roughly two kilograms in a small female brown pelican to perhaps fifteen kilograms in a heavy male Dalmatian pelican, with the six species in between covering the spread. Those are large numbers for flying birds; among living flying birds only the great albatrosses, some swans, bustards, and condors reach comparable masses. Big birds cannot afford wasteful flight, so the pelican wing is long, broad, and built for soaring and gliding more than for continuous flapping. In level cruise pelicans commonly move at speeds around fifty to sixty kilometers per hour, and they stretch that speed over enormous distances by riding air that other birds cannot use. They climb in thermals, the rising bubbles of warm air over land, and circle in them the way raptors do. They also work the wind gradients over waves and coastlines, and observations of brown pelicans suggest they exploit the upward deflection of air over wave crests to glide meter after meter without a wingbeat.

The white pelicans of the Americas have refined this to a communal art. A flock of American white pelicans climbs together in a morning thermal, forms the familiar V, and then moves across the landscape in long glides, each bird flying in the lifted air off the wingtip of the bird ahead, the same trick that lets airliner pilots save fuel in formation. Watching a pelican line come over the horizon at eye level, unmoving, silent, and utterly unhurried, is one of the great sights of inland birding.

Underwater and on the water, the body changes roles. The feet are fully webbed between all four front toes, a design shared with cormorants, gannets, and boobies, giving powerful paddling and a decent footing on soft mud. The legs are set far back on the body, which is ideal for swimming and terrible for walking; pelicans on land move with a rolling, flat-footed waddle that has done much for their comic reputation. In the plunge-divers, a network of subcutaneous air sacs under the skin of the breast, flanks, and neck cushions the impact of the dive, spreads the deceleration load, and provides buoyancy for the swim back to the surface with a full pouch. A brown pelican hitting the water from ten meters or more is briefly one of the most heavily loaded structures in the animal kingdom, and the air-sac system is why it can do it thousands of times without injury.

The skeleton carries the usual bird lightening: hollow, strut-braced bones. One detail is peculiarly pelican. The nostrils are essentially sealed; pelicans breathe through the open mouth and the internal nostril openings, an adaptation that keeps water out of the nasal passages during strikes and dives. This is why a pelican at rest so often sits with the bill slightly agape, apparently panting. It is not necessarily hot. It may simply be breathing the way its family has breathed for thirty million years.

Feathers and skin complete the waterproof suit. Pelicans preen copiously, drawing each feather through the bill and distributing oil from the uropygial gland at the base of the tail. Plumage is mostly white or gray-brown depending on species, and the white species achieve their clean look with constant maintenance; pelicans are among the most dedicated bathers in the bird world, and a group-fisher flock loafing between fishing bouts will often break off, one by one, for a bath, a wing-flapping splash, and a return to preening. Feathers are renewed in an annual molt, staggered so the bird is never grounded.

Two more pieces of plumbing deserve mention. Pelicans have functional salt glands above the eyes, which pull excess salt from the blood and excrete it as a drip from the nostril area, letting marine-living species cope with a salty diet and seawater intake. And the digestive tract is a straightforward fish processor with a twist: pelicans, like their cormorant relatives, produce pellets, coughing up compacted bundles of bones and scales after the soft tissue has been digested. On a colony island, weeks of pelican meals lie around as gray felted pellets under the nests, and researchers sieve them to reconstruct the local fish fauna without ever wetting a net.

Put the pieces together and the pelican makes sense as a design brief executed to the last line item: a body heavy enough to store the fat and muscle that long migrations and hard breeding seasons demand, a wing big enough to move that body almost for free across a continent, feet and air sacs that let the same body hunt at sea, and a pouch that turns a lunging mouth into a net. The comic outline is a human projection. The animal underneath is one of the most thoroughly optimized machines in its size class anywhere on earth.

Several patterns deserve a moment before the individual portraits begin.

First, the split between the two "white" pelicans of the Old and New Worlds is a story of geography, not behavior. Great white and American white pelicans look strikingly alike, feed the same way, breed in similar inland colonies, and would probably be mistaken for each other by anyone shown only museum skins of soft parts. Their common ancestor's range was split, probably by Pleistocene climate and water-level swings, and the two populations went their own ways on opposite sides of the world. The visible difference is modest: the American species has black flight feathers visible as a dark trailing edge in flight, while the great white shows black only on the outermost wing tip. Everything else is a matter of habitat details.

Second, the brown and Peruvian pair shows the same story at sea. The Peruvian pelican was treated for most of the twentieth century as a subspecies of the brown. It is larger, darker above, and lives in the cold, explosively productive Humboldt Current off Peru and Chile, but its breeding biology and its plunge-dive are near copies of the Atlantic brown pelican's. Genetics settled what field guides had long suspected: they are separate species that diverged after the rise of the modern Humboldt ecosystem.

Third, the two Near Threatened species, the Dalmatian and the spot-billed, are both Old World inland birds whose troubles come less from the open ocean than from wetland drainage, disturbance, and now disease. Both are discussed at length in later chapters, and both are conservation stories still being written.

Fourth, the Australian pelican deserves its reputation as the family's opportunist. It is the species with the longest bill of any living bird, with large males approaching half a meter, and it lives a nomadic boom-and-bust life across the driest inhabited continent, breeding in tens of thousands when inland lakes flood and dispersing to the coasts when they dry. More than any other species it demonstrates the pelican design's real secret: the ability to go nowhere fast and eat nothing much for extended periods, then convert a single great fish bloom into a generation of chicks.

Finally, notice how small the brown pelican looks in this company. The species most people picture when they hear the word pelican, gliding down a Florida beach in loose line, is actually the runt of the family, a specialized marine hunter that traded size for agility. Its smallness is not a defect. It is the adaptation that lets a bird with a meter and a half of wings dive from the air, which its heavier cousins cannot safely do. In pelican evolution, as in economics, different sizes buy different markets, and all eight species have found buyers.

With the map drawn, the next eight chapters visit each species in its own country.
## Chapter 6. The Great White Pelican

The great white pelican is the bird of the medieval emblem and the London park, the species whose scientific name became the name of the whole family. It is also, by range and numbers, the world's most widespread pelican, breeding from southeastern Europe across the Middle East and Central Asia to Mongolia and China, and down the length of Africa wherever large shallow waters exist. Its total population is estimated on the order of a quarter of a million birds, and it is assessed as Least Concern, though local colonies rise and fall dramatically and a few of the most famous ones have vanished in living memory.

A great white in breeding plumage is one of the handsomest sights in ornithology. The body is clean white with a rose blush, strongest on the back and breast, and long curved feathers of the nape form a loose crest. The pouch at the height of the season flushes pink, and the bare skin around the eye takes on yellow and blue tones. In flight the wings are very long and appear almost rectangular, with the outermost black primaries standing out as a neat dark tip against otherwise pale wings.

The species is a bird of shallow, fish-rich water: Rift Valley lakes, river deltas, coastal lagoons, man-made reservoirs, and flooded plains. Its strongholds include the Danube Delta and other Black Sea wetlands, the lakes of eastern Africa, and, in the south, the lagoons of the Namibian coast, where great whites and pink-backed pelicans share artificial guano platforms that people built for harvesting seabird droppings decades ago. Large colonies form on low islands and spits, where a scraping nest holds one to three eggs, more commonly two.

Behaviorally, the great white is the archetype of the cooperative fisher. On a good morning a flock spreads into a loose arc or line and drives fish before it, wings half spread to form a moving fence, then the whole formation closes and every bird lunges at once, pouches forward. Observers on Rift Valley lakes have described synchronized drives involving hundreds of birds that seem to require at least some agreement about timing. The species also feeds at night on occasion, particularly where daytime disturbance is heavy, and where it lives near fishing ports it learns the schedule of the boats.

The great white pelican has a documented taste for the unusual when city life gives it the chance. Around Cape Town, South Africa, birds in city parks and wetlands learned to prey on feral pigeons, herding and snatching them at the water's edge, a behavior recorded by researchers and now part of the local tourist routine. On colony islands, cannibalism of chicks, usually by non-breeding or stressed adults, has also been documented, as it has in several other pelican species; colonial life has a dark ledger as well as a warm one.

Culturally this is the species with the longest paper trail. The Hebrew Bible lists a pelican among the unclean birds, and the psalmist compares his desolation to that of the pelican in the wilderness. Greek and Roman writers describe the bird feeding its young from its breast, a misreading of real behavior that grew into the medieval symbol of Christ-like sacrifice and, in heraldry, the pelican in her piety. Queen Elizabeth I chose to be painted as a pelican in the 1570s, feeding her people from her own body. For most of that long history, the actual bird behind the symbol was the great white of European and Levantine wetlands, the pelican of the Danube and the Jordan, the bird European artists could see.

Today the species remains secure in most of Africa but faces a patchier picture in the north. The famous colony in the Danube Delta was destroyed by flooding and disturbance in the twentieth century and has rebuilt with protection; the wetlands of Turkey and the Middle East have been drained or degraded; and collisions with power lines and entanglement in fishing gear kill pelicans in numbers wherever cheap electricity and cheap nets meet big birds. None of this yet threatens the species as a whole, but the story of the great white, like that of several of its relatives, is a story of a bird that is everywhere in one decade and locally missing the next. Wetlands are easy to lose and slow to regain, and pelicans need wetlands the way cities need harbors.
## Chapter 7. The American White Pelican

The American white pelican is the great unappreciated spectacle of the North American interior. Tourists fly south to see brown pelicans dive off Florida piers, while a fifty-meter line of American whites crosses the sky over a prairie lake with more total bird per bird than any brown pelican ever carried. The species breeds in scattered inland colonies from British Columbia and the prairie provinces of Canada south through the Dakotas, Minnesota, and the mountain lakes of the western United States, and winters on the Pacific coast, the Gulf coast, the lower Mississippi valley, and Mexico.

In size it is a serious animal. Length runs to a meter and a half or more, wingspans commonly reach between 2.4 and 2.9 meters, and large males approach nine kilograms. In the hand of the wind, all that bulk disappears. American whites are superb soarers, and during migration they move in the long silent V formations that give prairie skies their signature; individual flocks have been tracked riding a single thermal to altitude and then gliding for many kilometers without a beat.

The species nests colonially on isolated islands in large lakes, marshes, and reservoirs, with some colonies holding many thousands of pairs. Chase Lake in North Dakota supports one of the largest gatherings, and colony sites are traditional, sometimes used for decades. The nest is a scrape, the clutch usually two eggs, and incubation lasts about a month. Like other pelicans, whites frequently cover the eggs with their webbed feet rather than sitting directly on them, an old observation confirmed repeatedly by nest cameras. Both parents incubate and both feed the young by regurgitation, the chicks reaching into the parent's open bill and pouch.

Two behaviors define the species in the public eye. The first is cooperative fishing. American whites herd fish in groups, swimming in coordinated lines or arcs that concentrate small fish into shallows, then feeding together in a synchronized rush. The second is theft. Whites are enthusiastic kleptoparasites and will shadow double-crested cormorants, grebes, and other divers, waiting at the surface to snatch prey as the diver comes up. A cormorant surfacing with a fish is a cormorant running a gauntlet. Gulls do the same to pelicans, perching on their heads and bills at the moment of swallowing, so that on a winter Gulf beach the feeding chain reads like a heist comedy: the pelican robs the cormorant, the gull robs the pelican, and everybody goes home lighter.

The species has one very human vulnerability. Colony islands are sensitive, and whites are notorious for abandoning nests en masse after disturbance; a colony can lose a whole year's breeding to a single bad day of human intrusion, whether that intrusion is an off-road vehicle, a drone, or a survey crew that came too close. Management of the big colonies is largely about keeping people out during the nesting months, and the colonies that are protected have, in most years, grown.

The population story of the last half century is one of the quiet successes of North American conservation. Pressured historically by shooting, egging, and wetland loss, and later by pesticide contamination, American white pelicans dropped to low numbers early in the twentieth century, then recovered steadily through the century's second half as refuges were established and attitudes changed. Today the species numbers well over a hundred thousand birds and continues to expand into new breeding areas, including man-made reservoirs that did not exist when Audubon painted its portrait.

It is fitting that this most American of pelicans is the one least tied to the sea. It lives in the middle of the continent, on lakes that freeze, in a landscape of grain elevators and gas flares, and it makes that landscape work by treating every flooded field and every reservoir as a stage of its nomadic calendar. When the water is right, a prairie pothole that last held pelicans in 1987 can hold ten thousand of them again the next spring. No other pelican makes a home of places so thoroughly given over to people, and few do it with less fuss.
## Chapter 8. The Brown Pelican

The brown pelican is the pelican of the postcard, the mascot, the state bird, and the beach. From the Carolinas around the Gulf of Mexico and down through the Caribbean, and along the Pacific coast from British Columbia to Central America, it is the large dark bird that cruises just above the waves in loose lines, folds its wings, and falls into the sea. It is also the pelican with the hardest conservation story, which makes its current abundance one of the great, if imperfect, environmental recoveries of the twentieth century.

Six subspecies are commonly recognized, from the Atlantic and Gulf coasts of the United States through the Caribbean, the Pacific coast of the Americas, the Galapagos, and the Pacific coast of Colombia and Ecuador. Among pelicans the brown is a specialist: it is the family's smallest member, it is almost strictly marine, and it is one of only two species that catch fish by plunging from the air.

The plunge-dive deserves description, because it is one of the most dramatic hunting moves in the bird world. A brown pelican patrols at heights from a few meters to twenty meters or more, looking down. When it spots a fish, often an anchovy, sardine, mullet, or menhaden near the surface, it banks, stalls, and drops, rotating slightly so that it hits the water bill-first in a spray visible a hundred meters away. The impact is absorbed by the air sacs under the skin and by the pouch, which everts forward underwater and traps the fish in a rush of displaced water. The bird surfaces, drains the pouch with a practiced backward tilt, swallows, takes off with heavy flaps, climbs, and does it again. Gulls know all about this and habitually station themselves on the pelican's head and bill, stealing scraps and harassing the swallow. The brown pelican tolerates the piracy with the weary patience of an employee whose bag is searched at the exit every day.

The conservation story begins with DDT. The synthetic pesticide, used heavily after the Second World War, entered aquatic food chains and was concentrated in fish. In pelicans it interfered with calcium metabolism, and eggshells thinned so badly that eggs collapsed under the weight of incubating parents. Brown pelican breeding collapsed across the Gulf coast and California in the 1950s and 1960s; in California, breeding effectively ceased by the 1940s and did not resume until 1980, on Anacapa Island in the Channel Islands, after DDT was banned in the United States in 1972. The species was listed under early federal endangered species legislation in 1970. Louisiana, whose state bird it is and whose seal shows a mother pelican feeding her young, had lost its breeding pelicans entirely and rebuilt them with a reintroduction program that moved more than a thousand chicks from Florida into coastal refuges between the late 1960s and the end of the 1970s.

Recovery was slow, then remarkable. Eggshell thickness returned toward normal as DDT levels fell, colonies re-established up and down the coasts, and by the first decade of the 2000s the brown pelican was again one of the most familiar birds on American subtropical shores. The US population was declared recovered and removed from the endangered species list, with the final delisting in 2009. Total population is now estimated in the hundreds of thousands.

Two years later came the test. The Deepwater Horizon blowout in April 2010 sent oil into the Louisiana marshes where many brown pelicans nest. The species, freshly delisted and freshly symbolic, became the face of the spill: oiled adults were captured by the hundred, washed, banded, and released on the Atlantic coast, and the long-term effects on the Gulf population are still being studied. The bird survived, but the episode demonstrated how concentrated its risks are. A species that nests on a few thousand low marsh islands and forages in coastal waters is exposed, in one bad season, to exactly the industrial accidents that coastal development makes likely.

For all that, the brown pelican today is a conservation success in plain sight, loafing on dock pilings, diving off piers, and raising chicks on mangrove islands within sight of beach umbrellas. It has also become, more than any other pelican, a patient of the rehabilitation world. Hooks in the pouch, line wraps, and boat strikes bring thousands of brown pelicans through rescue centers every year, and most are treated and released. The species that Americans nearly lost twice, to poison and to oil, is now the one that ordinary people most often save individually, one cut fishing line at a time. There is a lesson in that symmetry somewhere, and Chapter 19 returns to it.
## Chapter 9. The Peruvian Pelican

Fifty kilometers off the coast of Peru, the sea is green. Cold, nutrient-rich water wells up from the depths along the Humboldt Current, feeds a runaway bloom of plankton, and the plankton feeds anchoveta, a small fish that historically schooled in numbers that stagger the imagination. The anchoveta feeds everything: sea lions, penguins, cormorants, boobies, and the second of the world's two plunge-diving pelicans, the Peruvian pelican.

Taxonomically the species is a recent split. Through most of the twentieth century it was written off as a Pacific subspecies of the brown pelican, and the resemblance is real: same silhouette, same habit of gliding in lines just above the swells, same headlong crash into the sea. But the Peruvian bird is larger and heavier, dark gray-brown above with a white stripe up the neck, and it lives a very different economic life. Where the brown pelican ekes out a living over a long coastline, the Peruvian pelican sits on top of the richest upwelling system in the world ocean and, in good years, breeds in enormous numbers on the guano islands off the Peruvian and Chilean coasts.

Those islands deserve their own paragraph, because they are part of world economic history. Seabird droppings accumulated for centuries on the arid islands of the Humboldt system into deep, valuable layers of guano, and in the nineteenth century guano became Peru's great export, shipped to European and American farms as fertilizer. Pelicans nested alongside boobies and cormorants in the guano colonies, and the harvesters, whatever their faults, learned to manage the islands with guards and closed seasons because the birds were money. The legacy of that era is an infrastructure of artificial nesting platforms and guarded islands that pelicans still use.

The price of life atop an upwelling system is volatility. Every few years the equatorial Pacific warms in an El Nino event, upwelling weakens or slides offshore, the anchoveta scatter or vanish, and the Humboldt's seabirds face famine. In strong El Nino years Peruvian pelicans suffer near-total breeding failure, adults desert colonies, and mortality among weakened birds can be severe; populations crash by large fractions and then rebuild over the following productive years as the anchoveta return. The species is therefore assessed as Least Concern overall, but with a population that behaves less like a stable number and more like a wave that repeatedly rises and breaks.

Two recent pressures have changed the texture of that old boom-and-bust. The first is the anchoveta fishery itself, the largest single-species fishery on earth by tonnage most years, which competes directly with the pelican for its prey and has had to adopt quotas and closures to keep the system from being fished down. The second, discussed fully in Chapter 19, is highly pathogenic avian influenza, which tore through Peru's coastal birds in the waves beginning in late 2022 and killed pelicans and other seabirds in the thousands.

The Peruvian pelican has one more claim on attention: it is the least-documented pelican. Its remote colony islands, its boom-and-bust demography, and its overlap with a heavy-duty commercial fishery make tidy population assessments difficult, and basic parameters of its breeding biology in the wild remain less studied than those of its famous Atlantic cousin. For students of seabirds, it is the family's open frontier.
## Chapter 10. The Australian Pelican

The Australian pelican is the family's philosopher: patient, opportunistic, and apparently untroubled by the idea that the world may not cooperate. It breeds when and where the driest inhabited continent decides to flood, and it waits, sometimes for years, sometimes for decades, for that to happen. It also owns the longest bill of any living bird, with large males reaching about half a meter from bill tip to pouch, a record held with what appears to be complete indifference.

The species ranges across Australia, Tasmania, New Guinea, and into Indonesia, and occurs on almost any large water body in that enormous area: coastal lagoons, harbors, rivers, sewerage ponds, inland salt lakes, and desert playas after rain. In the coastal cities it is a familiar professional beggar, loafing on wharfs behind fishing boats; in the interior it is a phantom, appearing on lakes that were dry sand a month before.

The biology that makes this possible is nomadism on a continental scale. Pelicans follow water. When inland Australia's episodic flooding fills the great basins, most famously Lake Eyre in the center of the continent, fish and invertebrates hatch in explosive numbers, and pelicans arrive, often within weeks, from coasts a thousand or more kilometers away. Breeding colonies form on the flooded lake's islands, and in a good event the counts run to tens of thousands of nests. When the lake dries, the colony vanishes, the juveniles disperse, and the population melts back into the coastal flocks to wait. Banding has recovered Australian pelicans thousands of kilometers from where they were marked as chicks, including birds that crossed from the continent's center to New Guinea.

Lake Eyre itself fills fully only a few times a century, and when it does, the scene is one of the strangest in world birding: tens of thousands of pelicans nesting on what is usually desert, feeding on fish that will die when the water does, raising chicks on a clock that has to beat the evaporation. The colony is a gamble against arithmetic. In floods that end early, chicks are stranded on drying islands and die in numbers that make hard viewing; in floods that last, an entire generation is produced from nothing.

Around the coasts the species lives more conventionally and, it must be said, more shamelessly. Australian pelicans are renowned scavengers and robbers around fishing operations, and there are famous individual birds at particular fish markets and jetties whose names are better known than some of the local councillors. In Sydney and Adelaide they pose for photographs. In Western Australia they have, in at least one widely reported case, learned to prey on pigeons in city parks, in the same way their Old World cousins have in Cape Town, a nice piece of convergent urban opportunism.

Culturally the species owns Australia's most beloved bird character. In Colin Thiele's 1964 novel Storm Boy and its film versions, the boy's pelican companion Mr Percival became a national icon, and generations of Australians have considered the pelican a friend rather than a curiosity. Rescue and release groups up and down the coast treat pelican hookings and entanglements as routine work. The species is Least Concern and abundant, but like all large waterbirds it depends on people continuing to tolerate a two-meter bird that stares into the back of the boat, and the tolerance, so far, is one of Australian culture's steady features.
## Chapter 11. The Dalmatian Pelican

The Dalmatian pelican is the largest of the family and, by the measurements that matter to birders, one of the largest flying birds on earth. Length reaches a meter and three quarters or more, weight in heavy males can approach fifteen kilograms, and wingspan in the biggest birds is credibly put near three and a half meters, dimensions that put this species in the same conversation as the wandering albatross, with the important difference that the albatross is a gliding specialist of open ocean winds while the Dalmatian pelican is a wetland bird that flaps like a pelican, that is, heavily, slowly, and often.

The plumage is silver-white with a wash of gray, and in breeding condition the bird grows curly, disheveled nape feathers that give the species its name, crispus meaning curled. The pouch turns orange-red with a conspicuous yellow pouch ring at the height of the season, and the overall impression is less of a white bird than of a prehistoric one. It breeds patchily from southeastern Europe through Turkey, the Caucasus, and Central Asia to Mongolia and China, in freshwater marshes, river deltas, and large lakes, often breeding among reedbeds rather than on open sand.

The numbers tell a harder story. The global population is on the order of ten to fourteen thousand individuals, concentrated in a modest number of colonies, and the species is assessed as Near Threatened. Its largest stronghold is the Prespa Lakes region shared by Greece, Albania, and North Macedonia, where well over a thousand pairs nest in some years, supported by an unusual alliance of conservationists, fishermen, and, in hard winters, fish donated by the local fishing cooperatives to keep weakened birds alive. Other important colonies sit on the Danube Delta, in Turkish wetlands, in the Caspian region, and at lakes in Mongolia and China. In several of these places the species has recolonized former sites as protection improved, a slow, encouraging trend.

The species' troubles are the standard wetland list: drainage and water regulation, disturbance at colonies, illegal killing in parts of its range, collisions with power lines, and the fragility of reedbed and shallow-lake habitat in an intensively used landscape. To that list has been added disease. The waves of highly pathogenic avian influenza that began moving through wild birds in the early 2020s hit Dalmatian pelicans with particular severity, with mass mortality at Greek colonies in 2022 and die-offs elsewhere in the Balkans and beyond. A species whose entire population would fit comfortably into a few large towns cannot absorb such losses casually, and the flu waves remain the most serious new threat to its recovery.

Watching a Dalmatian pelican is one of European birding's great experiences, and it is increasingly accessible: the Prespa lakes, Lake Kerkini in northern Greece, and the Danube Delta all support the species in situations where careful visitors can see it well from boats or observation points. On a cold misty morning at Kerkini, Dalmatian pelicans drift past the moored boats at close range, curly-headed and prehistoric, sometimes close enough to see the water beading on the pouch feathers. They are, in the end, the pelican that most rewards the person willing to travel to see one, and among European conservationists they have become something of a flagship: the argument, made in feathers, that the old flooded places are worth keeping flooded.
## Chapter 12. The Pink-Backed Pelican

The pink-backed pelican is the family's quiet member, the smallest of the Old World species and the one most likely to be overlooked in favor of its larger relatives. It breeds across sub-Saharan Africa, from Senegal east to Ethiopia and south through East Africa to South Africa, with an outlying population in Madagascar, and it is the African pelican of the trees: unlike the ground-nesting whites, it typically nests in trees, standing water permitting, building a bulky stick nest in a fig, a mangrove, or any other substantial waterside tree.

The plumage is gray-white with, as the name promises, a pinkish tinge along the back, brightest in breeding condition, when the pouch also flushes yellowish and the bare facial skin takes on pink and gray tones. It is a slightly built pelican by the standards of the genus, with a wingspan around two meters and a weight usually between four and seven kilograms, and it carries those dimensions with a delicacy the great whites lack.

Ecologically it resembles the other cooperative fishers, feeding in flocks on shallow lakes, pans, floodplains, and coastal lagoons, often alongside great white pelicans, with which it generally maintains a professional distance rather than a rivalry: the bigger species takes the bigger fish and the deeper water, and the two species commonly share a lagoon without much visible conflict. Like its relatives it will take advantage of human fishing, following boats, robbing nets, and loitering at fish landing sites, and in some West African towns it is a familiar citizen of the wharf.

The stronghold of the species is hard to state precisely, because its colonies are small, mobile, and often ephemeral, shifting with floods and water levels across an enormous range. Colonies form at inland floodplains in the Sudd and the Niger inland delta regions, at East African lakes, and at southern African sites, including, most famously for birders, the man-made guano platforms at Walvis Bay on the Namibian coast, where pink-backed pelicans nest side by side with great whites on structures originally built for harvesting cormorant guano. The species is assessed as Least Concern, with a population commonly estimated in the hundreds of thousands, though the true figure is genuinely uncertain and local colonies rise and fall with the rains.

The pink-backed pelican's relationship with trees shapes its conservation profile in ways the ground nesters never face. A tree colony needs old trees that stand in or beside water and that remain flooded, or at least undisturbed, through the breeding season. Deforestation of waterside woodland, lowering of water tables, and human harvest of nesting trees all remove breeding sites silently, colony by colony, in a way that is easy to miss from year to year. In parts of its range the species has shifted to nesting on artificial structures, including ruined buildings and platform sites, a reminder that for a flexible species the important resource is not the tree itself but the combination of safety from ground predators and access to fish.

Among pelican enthusiasts the species has a particular charm: it is the pelican of the African dawn, feeding in the pink half-light on a floodplain that the day's heat will begin to bake within hours, silent except for the soft grunt and bill-clatter of the flock, and gone, often, by mid-morning to loaf in the shade of the trees where, come the next rains, it will build its stick nest and raise the next generation of the family's quiet members.
## Chapter 13. The Spot-Billed Pelican

The spot-billed pelican, also called the grey pelican, is the family's Asian representative and one of its rarest. It breeds patchily in South and Southeast Asia, with the most important remaining populations in India, Sri Lanka, Cambodia, and scattered sites in Myanmar, Thailand, and Indonesia, and it is assessed as Near Threatened, with a population generally estimated in the low tens of thousands and concentrated in a handful of colonies.

The name describes the bird at close range: the huge bill carries a row of dark spots along the edge of the upper mandible, a detail invisible at distance but unmistakable in photographs. The plumage is gray-white rather than the clean white of its cousins, with a grayish crest of long feathers down the nape, and the pouch in breeding condition turns pinkish with dark markings. It is a bird of lowland wetlands, reservoirs, and flooded timber, and, unlike most of its relatives, it is one of the species that nests most comfortably in the middle of human landscapes.

That last trait produces one of the loveliest conservation stories in this whole article. In the southern Indian state of Karnataka lies the village of Kokkare Bellur, whose name means pelican village in the local language. For generations spot-billed pelicans, together with painted storks and other waterbirds, have nested in the tall trees that stand in and around the village itself, above the rooftops, above the streets, above the daily life of several thousand people. The villagers regard the birds as lucky and traditionally protected them, collected the droppings as fertilizer, and treated the colony as part of the village's identity rather than as a nuisance. In an era when most colonial waterbirds flee human presence, Kokkare Bellur is the standing counterexample: a place where the pelicans and the people simply came to an arrangement centuries ago and have never seen a reason to end it.

Not everything about the arrangement is comfortable. The colony has fluctuated with disease, disturbance, tree loss, and changes in local fishing, and conservation groups and villagers now work together on monitoring and protection. A second major Indian site, the Nelapattu sanctuary in Andhra Pradesh, protects one of the largest breeding aggregations in the country, and in Southeast Asia the Tonle Sap lake system in Cambodia holds a significant population whose fortunes track the health of the flooded forest.

Elsewhere in its former range the species has fared worse. It once bred across a much wider swath of South and Southeast Asia, and populations in several countries have dwindled or vanished as wetlands were drained and colonies were disturbed. The species' habit of nesting in trees near villages cut both ways historically: proximity to people gave some colonies protection but left them vulnerable to felling, egg collection, and, in the modern era, to the simple fact that trees beside villages tend to be cut down eventually.

The spot-billed pelican thus holds a specific place in this article's argument. Chapter 19 will make the case that pelican conservation is mostly wetland conservation plus disturbance management, and Kokkare Bellur is the proof that the disturbance half is fundamentally social. The technology of protecting a pelican colony is simple: guard the site, leave the trees standing, keep the water clean and the fish coming. What is hard is the agreement. The Indian pelican village shows that the agreement can hold for centuries when the bird is woven into how a community sees itself, and it stands as both model and reproach to the places where pelicans were never given that chance.
## Chapter 14. Feeding I: The Plunge-Divers

Watch a brown pelican feed and you are watching the most athletic thing the family does. The plunge-dive is a full-body collision with the ocean, repeated dozens of times a day, and it works. This chapter takes the move apart.

The hunter patrols at altitude, from a few meters over calm water to twenty meters or more, usually flying into the wind, in loose lines or alone, scanning downward. The target is a fish near the surface: anchovies, sardines, herring, mullet, menhaden, mackerel, whatever schools shallow enough to be seen from the air. Pelicans also feed behind fishing boats, at river mouths, and in the churned water of channels where current concentrates bait. When a strike is chosen, the bird banks, then stalls and folds: wings swept to the body, neck extended, body rotating so the bill meets the water first, entry angle steep, closing from cruise speed to a stopped bird in the time it takes to fall ten meters.

The entry is engineered in three stages. The subcutaneous air sacs under the breast and flank skin absorb the shock, spreading deceleration over the thorax like an airbag. The neck, thick-muscled and straight, transmits the impact down the body's long axis rather than letting it snap the cervical vertebrae sideways. And the pouch does its trick underwater: the lower jaw rami flex apart, the pouch everts forward, and the forward motion of the body drives a slug of water and fish into the open bag. The bird, now briefly a submarine, rises buoyed by its trapped air sacs, surfaces, and deals with the catch.

Dealing with the catch is the comic half of the performance. The pouch now contains, in a typical catch, a few hundred grams of fish in several liters of water. The pelican cannot swallow all that, so it drains: head tilted back and slightly to one side, pouch contracted in peristaltic waves, water forced out through the corners of the mouth while the tongue and pouch floor hold the fish back. The sequence takes seconds. Then the head snaps forward and the fish goes down whole, head first.

The physics of the plunge impose constraints, and the species' biology follows them. Diving from height only makes sense where fish are visible from the air, so plunge-divers are birds of clear or lightly turbid coastal water and surface-schooling fish, not of muddy rivers or deep lakes. Impact energy scales with mass and height, so plunge-divers are the smallest pelicans, two to seven kilograms, not twelve. And visibility is a daily lottery, so both species supplement the dive with opportunistic feeding: robbing other birds, scavenging discards behind boats, and, in the Peruvian pelican's case, shifting local effort as the anchoveta move.

The Peruvian pelican's version of the life differs mainly in scale of opportunity. Over the Humboldt Current the same dive, executed over the richest surface fishery in the world, yields catches in a different class, and Peruvian pelicans often feed in sizable groups, line after line of birds folding into the sea over a bait ball. Brown pelicans also feed socially on occasion, particularly over large anchovy or menhaden schools, where dozens of birds strike the same school in waves.

Two parasitic sidelines complete the picture. Gulls of several species have made a profession of meeting surfacing brown pelicans at the bill, perching on head or pouch and snatching fish as the pelican swallows, an arrangement pelicans resist without much conviction. And both plunge-divers will rob cormorants, a neat reversal of the usual order of seabird grudges. In the economy of the inshore sea, the plunge-divers are the visible entrepreneurs, and everyone else lines up for the spillage.
## Chapter 15. Feeding II: The Group Fishers

The six surface-feeding pelicans do everything the plunge-divers do, except the falling. They hunt while swimming, at the surface, and most of them do it in groups whose coordination looks, at its best, like something designed. This chapter covers how group fishing actually works, what pelicans eat when they are not being filmed being dignified, and the ecological politics of feeding in a flock of two-meter birds.

The signature maneuver is the drive. A flock feeding over shallow water spreads into a loose arc, line, or U, and begins to advance, each bird swimming forward with wings partly spread, the tips often dragging the water. The formation herds small fish ahead of it into shallower, tighter water, a moving seine of birds. At some coordinated moment the arc closes and the whole flock lunges, heads down, pouches out, and the water in front of them disappears into a row of straining bags. Each bird surfaces with its own catch, drains, swallows, and the flock reforms for the next drive. Great white pelicans on Rift Valley lakes, American whites on prairie marshes, and Dalmatian pelicans on Balkan lake shallows all perform recognizably similar versions, and observers since the nineteenth century have argued about how much of the coordination is true cooperation and how much is each bird simply doing well out of the confusion that neighbors create. The honest answer is probably both: the fish are clearly easier to catch inside the formation, and no individual gains by defecting from it, which is the standard recipe for stable cooperation without any committee ever having met.

Group fishing imposes its own etiquette, and pelicans violate it constantly. Kleptoparasitism, theft of caught fish, runs in every direction in a mixed flock. Whites rob cormorants and grebes. Gulls rob everybody. Pelicans rob gulls back when the opportunity is blatant, and they will scavenge carrion and fish scraps, follow fishing boats, and clean up at fish landing sites. On inland Africa, great whites feed at night in places where daytime heat or disturbance is heavy. The species is flexible to the point of cynicism, and that flexibility is a large part of the family's success across so many kinds of water.

What is on the menu? Chiefly small, schooling, surface-oriented fish, typically a few centimeters to a hand's length: anchovies and sardines in the sea, tilapia and cyprinids inland, mullet in estuaries. Around that core the list widens by opportunity. Crustaceans and amphibians are taken where fish are scarce. The famous outrider cases are worth stating plainly, because they show the design's real reach. Great white pelicans in Cape Town's urban wetlands have learned to prey on feral pigeons, ambushing them at the water's edge, and have been photographed doing so repeatedly. Several species have been documented eating the chicks of other waterbirds at colonies, and cannibalism of their own species' chicks, chiefly by non-breeding or food-stressed adults, is recorded in the great white and others. An Australian pelican on a dry-out lake will eat whatever the water offers, up to and including small ducks. The pouch sets no ideological limits.

How much does a pelican eat? Working figures for brown pelicans are on the order of one to two kilograms of fish a day, and the larger species presumably scale up with body mass, meaning a big colony of thousands of pairs moves tonnes of fish daily during the breeding season. Colony islands are, among other things, the focal points of a food web: fish come in as chicks and leave as pellets of bone scattered under the nests, and the droppings fertilize the island's plants and the shallows around it. A pelican colony is an engine that converts open-water fish into concentrated island fertility, which is why fishermen in some cultures have historically regarded pelicanries the way farmers regard wild manure sources, and why the guano of Humboldt seabirds was once national wealth.

The group-fishing species thus live by a different contract from the plunge-divers: smaller individual strikes, cooperative and opportunistic, nocturnal when useful, tied to shallow productive water that people also value. It is a good contract in good years, and Chapters 18 and 19 follow what happens when the contract meets drainage ditches, pesticides, and a warming sea.
## Chapter 16. Breeding Colonies: Eggs, Chicks, and the Siblicide Problem

All eight pelicans are colonial breeders, and the colony is where the family's social life, its worst-kept secret, and its best conservation leverage all come together. This chapter walks through a pelican breeding season from pairing to independence, and then faces the part of the story that visitors to a colony find hardest: why so many nests end with one dead chick.

The season opens with arrival and display. Male and female pelicans look alike in the field in most species, with males averaging slightly larger, and pair bonds form within the colony, often on or near the nest site. Courtship is a quiet theater of pouches. Pairs face each other, bills clattering and grunting, heads swung in unison, pouches flushed with breeding color and presented like signage; males parade with open bills and others join and leave the displays, and within days a dense cluster of pairs forms the core of the colony that the rest fill in around. Nest sites are chosen with two criteria: food within commuting range, and ground or trees that terrestrial predators cannot easily work.

The nest itself is minimal by the family's standards of grandeur elsewhere. Ground-nesting species scrape a shallow bowl and line it lightly with whatever is at hand; tree-nesting species build a substantial platform of sticks. The clutch is small, one to three eggs in most species, and incubation runs about four weeks. Pelican incubation has a signature quirk noted by every serious observer since Audubon: the birds frequently cover the eggs with their webbed feet rather than sitting down on them directly, standing over the clutch with the warm webs pressed to the shells. Both sexes incubate and both continue in full partnership through chick rearing.

Eggs hatch over a spread of two or three days, and the hatch order matters more than anything else that happens in the nest. Pelican chicks emerge naked, pink, and helpless, then grow into one of ornithology's strangest-looking life stages: a creature like a pterodactyl stuffed into a sleeping bag, with an oversized bill sheath and a temperament fixed on food. The parents feed by regurgitation, the chick reaching its whole head into the parent's open bill and pouch to pull out semi-digested fish. Chicks form creches as they grow, huddling in groups on the colony ground while parents come and go on fishing trips, and the creche is what visitors most often see: a gravel bank covered in thousands of gray, cheeping bags with wings.

Now the hard part. In the brown pelican, and to a lesser but real degree in the great white and several others, the second chick almost never survives unless the first dies. The older, stronger sibling outcompetes the younger for the parents' regurgitated fish, and in ordinary years the younger chick starves within the first weeks, sometimes killed outright by its sibling. This is siblicide, and in the brown pelican it is close to obligate, a built-in, scheduled death rather than an accident of a bad year. The evolutionary logic, argued over since the 1960s, is the standard bet-hedging case: the second egg exists as insurance against the failure of the first, and the species has accepted the routine death of the insurance chick as the price of the policy. It is a hard thing to watch and it is not a malfunction. Still, the colony floor in a brown pelican colony in June tells the visitor something true about nature that no nature documentary has fully metabolized, and honesty about it is part of any serious account of the bird.

From hatching to first flight takes roughly ten to twelve weeks across the family, and the parents keep feeding the fledglings for some weeks after they can fly, teaching them the routes to the fishing grounds. Young pelicans disperse in their first year, often far, and most return to breed in their third or fourth year, younger birds sometimes making early breeding attempts that fail. Wild lifespans of twenty years and more are documented from banding, with captive birds living decades longer.

The colony, meanwhile, has been doing its quiet ecological work the whole season: tonnes of fish converted into birds, feathers, bone piles, and guano; the island's vegetation fertilized into lushness; the surrounding water grazed of small fish. Pelicanries smell like a fish dock crossed with a hay barn, and the smell is the smell of the family's one universally shared trait: it turns open water, efficiently and in enormous quantity, into more pelican. Everything else in this article is detail.
## Chapter 17. Migration, Dispersal, and Nomadism

Ask where a pelican is from and you have asked a question the bird has never considered. Pelicans are not attached to places the way, say, albatrosses are attached to islands. They are attached to fish, and they move at the scale the fish dictate. The eight species resolve into three movement strategies, and knowing which strategy a species uses explains most of its natural history.

The first strategy is classical migration, and the master is the American white pelican. It breeds on inland lakes of the North American interior and winters on the Pacific coast, the Gulf coast, and the southern rivers, moving in flocks between the two with a punctuality that lets compilers of Christmas bird counts set their watches. The migration is done cheaply: long glides in thermal-soaring lines, tens of individuals stacked in a single column, entire flocks crossing a thousand kilometers of continental interior on a fuel bill that a flapping bird of the same mass could not have met. Great white pelicans in the Palearctic practice a looser version, with northern breeders withdrawing toward the Mediterranean, the Nile, and South Asia in winter, and the Dalmatian pelican largely dispersive within its range, gathering at ice-free waters in hard winters.

The second strategy is post-breeding dispersal, the young bird's grand tour. Juvenile pelicans wander far beyond the breeding range, often hundreds or thousands of kilometers, in their first years before settling to breed. This is the mechanism behind most records of brown pelicans far north of their range on the Atlantic coast and the occasional bird that turns up far inland or on a wrong ocean, and it is why a patient birder on an unfashionable jetty has a nonzero chance of writing a rarity report.

The third strategy is the Australian pelican's, and it has no schedule at all. The species is a nomad at the scale of a continent, tracking water across interior Australia, breeding when the inland lakes flood and dispersing to the coasts when they dry. Banding recoveries have shown movements of thousands of kilometers, including from the center of the continent to New Guinea, and the timing of its breeding follows floods rather than seasons. The Peruvian pelican lives a fourth, stormier variant, its movements and breeding success tied to the anchoveta and the El Nino cycle, so that the population's center of gravity slides up and down the Humboldt with the ocean's temperature.

Two generalizations fall out of all this, and both matter for conservation. First, pelicans depend on networks, not points. A migratory American white needs the prairie breeding lake, the staging marshes, the winter estuary, and safe passage between them; lose any link and the chain fails quietly, a few years later, in numbers nobody voted on. Second, pelicans will find new habitat with astonishing speed when it appears. Reservoirs, flooded quarries, fish ponds, and restored wetlands have all been colonized within years of creation. The birds are standing by. The constraint is almost never the pelican's ability to move; it is the supply of functioning water with fish in it, and the tolerance of the people who own its edges.

One movement record deserves a final word because it captures the family's spirit. Australian banders working a Lake Eyre flooding event have recorded chicks that, years later, turned up in places no pelican was supposed to be, across deserts, over mountains, onto other nations' coasts. A bird with a brain weighing a few grams hatched on a desert lake that will be sand again within two years, and its species responded to that fact not by clinging to the lake but by building an animal that can leave, and leaving the decision of when to a fish counter somewhere in the bird's blood. That is the pelican's whole philosophy of geography, and it has been working for thirty million years.
## Chapter 18. Pelicans and People: A Long, Uneasy History

The pelican is one of the few animals whose cultural history is longer and stranger than its natural history. It entered human writing at the very beginning of human writing about birds, and it has never left. This chapter follows the record from the Bronze Age to the twentieth century; Chapter 20 picks up the story of the bird as a modern pop-culture object.

The oldest layer is scriptural. The Hebrew Bible names a bird translated as pelican in several places: among the unclean birds in Leviticus, as an image of lonely desolation in Psalm 102, where the writer compares himself to a pelican of the wilderness, and in Isaiah's vision of ruined Edom, given over to pelican and hedgehog. Whether the Hebrew word behind the translation always meant the bird we mean is a matter for philologists, but the tone stuck. In the ancient Near Eastern and Mediterranean mind, the pelican became a bird of ruins, solitude, and grief, huge and pale and standing alone in a dried marsh. Greek and Roman naturalists kept that tone and added a legend: that the pelican kills its rebellious young and then, three days later, pierces its own breast and revives them with its blood. The legend is a garbled reading of real behavior, probably of the red breeding pouch pressed to the nest and the visible blood color of the gular skin, and it became one of the most successful misreadings in the history of animals.

Medieval Christianity made it doctrine. The pelican that feeds her young from her own flesh, the pelican in her piety, became an emblem of Christ's sacrifice, carved on hundreds of English churches, painted on altar pieces, and set into the verses of the Eucharistic hymn Adoro te devote, attributed to Thomas Aquinas, which addresses Christ as the gentle pelican. The image entered heraldry, where a pelican vulning herself stands on coats of arms to this day. Queen Elizabeth I had herself painted as the pelican in Nicholas Hilliard's portrait of the 1570s, presenting the image to her subjects with a straightforward political meaning: the queen feeds her people with her own body. The pelican, a bird that actually feeds its young by regurgitating fish, thus became the mascot of sacrificial motherhood across half of Europe, and no other animal symbol has survived so long with so little attention paid to the animal behind it.

The real bird began to reenter the record with the age of observation. In North America, the painter and naturalist John James Audubon worked the brown pelican into The Birds of America in the 1830s, drawing the bird he had watched diving off the Florida keys and noting, with his usual patience, the details of its feeding and nesting. In London, pelicans returned to the capital's most visible pond in 1664, when a Russian ambassador is recorded as presenting great white pelicans to St James's Park, beginning a resident population that continues today and that produced, three and a half centuries later, the most famous wildlife video of the modern internet: a pelican strolling across the park and swallowing a live pigeon whole while bystanders filmed in disbelief. The video's viral career in the 2000s shocked viewers who knew the bird only from heraldry. The pelican, it turned out, had never signed the emblem.

The age of European exploration and naming scattered pelicans across the map of human geography. Spanish explorers in San Francisco Bay in the 1770s recorded the name that became Alcatraz, from a Portuguese and Spanish word for gannets and pelicans, itself borrowed through Arabic, naming the island for the seabirds that crowded it; the prison came much later and kept the bird's name. In 1903, an island off the Florida coast where brown pelicans nested became Pelican Island, protected by executive order of President Theodore Roosevelt as the first federal bird reservation in the United States, the founding act of what became the National Wildlife Refuge System. The pelican had become, before most people noticed, the founding bird of American conservation.

Not every meeting was gentle. Fisherfolk and pelicans have quarreled wherever both wanted the same net, and the quarrel has often ended in shooting, egging, and poisoning; colonial waterbird slaughter was ordinary sport and livelihood into the early twentieth century. In the twentieth century's second half, the quarrel modernized into disputes over fish farms and inland fisheries, which remain genuine management problems in parts of Africa, Asia, and North America today. And yet the counterexamples kept appearing: the Indian pelican village of Kokkare Bellur, where the birds have nested above the streets for centuries as honored neighbors; the fishing cooperative at Lake Kerkini in Greece, which feeds Dalmatian pelicans through cold winters; the Namibian guano platforms, built for an industry, colonized by the birds, and now a tourist spectacle.

The pattern across three thousand years is consistent. People have always found the pelican useful as a symbol first and tolerable as a neighbor second. The symbol is ancient and mostly about self-sacrifice; the neighbor is enormous, noisy, fishy, and occasionally outrageous. The conservation history of the family, taken up in the next chapter, is in large part the story of what happened when the real bird finally demanded the attention that the symbol had been absorbing for millennia.
## Chapter 19. Conservation: From DDT to Bird Flu

By the standards of large waterbirds, pelicans are doing well: six of the eight species are listed as Least Concern. But the two words behind that calm summary, "large" and "waterbird," hide the entire risk model. Pelicans are long-lived animals that reproduce slowly, and they concentrate by the thousands at a small number of breeding sites, most of them low, flat, and near water that people want for other purposes. A species that keeps most of its population in a handful of towns' worth of birds on a handful of islands is never more than one bad decade from trouble, and the twentieth century demonstrated this three separate times. This chapter reviews the hits, the recoveries, and the current ledger.

The first demonstration was the shotgun era. Through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, colonial waterbirds across North America, Africa, and Asia were shot for sport, harvested for eggs, feathers, and guano, and persecuted as competitors of fishermen. Numbers fell across the family's range, some colonies were destroyed outright, and the spot-billed pelican began the long decline that continues today. The remedy, where it was applied, was the one conservationists still use: guard the colony, protect the water, let the bird's own demography do the rest. Pelican Island in 1903 was the model case, and the model worked.

The second demonstration was chemical. DDT, the organochlorine pesticide deployed worldwide after the Second World War, entered aquatic food chains and concentrated in fish-eating birds. In pelicans, the DDT breakdown product DDE disrupted calcium deposition, eggshells thinned, and eggs failed under incubating adults. Brown pelican breeding collapsed across the southeastern United States and California in the 1950s and 1960s; the species was listed as endangered in 1970, and breeding in California, which had stopped decades earlier, did not resume until 1980 after the 1972 United States ban. Rachel Carson had used exactly this class of evidence in Silent Spring in 1962, and the brown pelican became one of the case histories that justified the entire regulatory architecture of pesticide control. Recovery was slow, then decisive: shells thickened, colonies re-formed, Louisiana's reintroduced population took hold, and in 2009 the species was declared recovered and removed from the list. It remains the family's flagship recovery.

The third demonstration came two years after the delisting. The Deepwater Horizon blowout of April 2010 sent millions of barrels of oil into the northern Gulf of Mexico, precisely across the brown pelican's densest breeding and wintering grounds. The freshly delisted state bird of Louisiana became the spill's emblem: hundreds of visibly oiled pelicans were captured, cleaned, and relocated, thousands more were affected in ways that never made a rescue tally, and the long-term demographic effects are still being quantified from the settlement-funded studies. The lesson was blunt. A delisting is a paper event, and a species whose entire way of life sits at the interface of sea, marsh, and heavy industry holds its recovery at the pleasure of that interface.

The current threats fit in a paragraph, and none of them are novel in kind. Bycatch and entanglement drown pelicans in nets and lines worldwide, and hook injuries fill rehabilitation centers in Florida, Australia, and South Africa. Power lines kill pelicans along migration routes and near colonies. Wetland drainage and water regulation continue to remove habitat in Asia, Africa, and Europe; the Aral and Mesopotamian systems, among others, no longer support what they once did. Disturbance at colonies, from boats, drones, and visitors, can abort a whole season in an afternoon. The anchoveta fishery of the Humboldt is managed under quotas, but it shares water with the Peruvian pelican, and in El Nino years the bird pays first. Climate change operates on all of these at once: it shifts fish, raises seas over low nesting islands, and changes the flood regimes that species like the Australian pelican bet their generations on.

And there is one new threat with old-fashioned virulence. The panzootic of highly pathogenic avian influenza that began spreading in wild birds in the early 2020s has hit pelicans hard in several regions. Mass die-offs of Dalmatian pelicans struck Greek colonies in 2022 and spread through Balkan wetlands; Peruvian and other Pacific seabirds died in the thousands beginning in late 2022; and the virus has appeared in pelican colonies elsewhere in the family's range. For abundant species these are grievous shocks, survivable but serious. For the Dalmatian pelican, with a global population of roughly ten to fourteen thousand, a virus that can kill hundreds at a single colony in a season is a conservation event of the first order, and surveillance, carcass removal, and colony protection are now standing parts of its management.

What works, works in known ways, and it is worth closing the chapter with the short list. Guarded colonies produce birds; Prespa, Nelapattu, and the guarded guano islands of Peru all prove it. Community agreements outlast enforcement; Kokkare Bellur has held for centuries. Clean fish and clean water produce chicks; Lake Kerkini's fishermen-feeding program and the recovery after DDT both say so. Honest fishery management shares the sea; the anchoveta quota system, whatever its strains, is the difference between a crash and a squeeze for the Peruvian pelican. And rescue infrastructure, the unglamorous network of seabird rehabilitation centers, converts thousands of individual pelican catastrophes per year into recoveries. Six of eight species are Least Concern because those tools were applied, and the two that are Near Threatened are Near Threatened because they were applied too late and too thinly where those birds live. The ledger is open, the tools are known, and the pelican, as always, is standing by.
## Chapter 20. The Pelican in Culture: Limericks, Books, Teams, and Brand Names

The twentieth century took the pelican out of the church and put it into popular culture, and the bird has thrived there. This chapter collects the modern appearances that most people have actually met, from the best-known poem in ornithology to the name on a laptop case.

The poem first, because everyone half knows it and most quote it wrong. The lines

A wonderful bird is the pelican,
His bill will hold more than his belican,
He can take in his beak
Food enough for a week,
But I'm damned if I see how the helican

are usually attributed to Ogden Nash, who would have been flattered but innocent. They were published in 1910 by Dixon Lanier Merritt, a Nashville newspaperman and humorist, and they have since done more for the pelican's popular image than a century of natural history writing. As Chapter 3 showed, the poem is also, allowing for the week, substantially correct: the pouch does hold more than the stomach, by a factor of roughly three. It may be the only limerick in history that survives rigorous peer review.

Literature claimed the bird early. In Australia, Colin Thiele's 1964 novel Storm Boy, filmed in 1976 and remade in 2019, made Mr Percival the pelican one of that country's most beloved fictional animals, and generations of Australians grew up on the boy-and-bird story of the Coorong. In the United States, John Grisham's 1992 legal thriller The Pelican Brief and its 1993 film used the bird's name for a document connecting oil money to murders, and the book's jacket bird glided through airports for a year. Animation added Nigel, the comic, sentimental pelican of Finding Nemo in 2003, who returned in the sequel. And Britain's St James's Park colony, resident since 1664, produced the viral pigeon video of the 2000s that rebranded the bird overnight from heraldic saint to unrepentant opportunist, a correction the ornithological record had been waiting to make since Isaiah.

Sport took the bird into its big leagues. New Orleans, whose state bird is the brown pelican and whose great seal shows the pelican feeding her young, renamed its NBA franchise the Pelicans in 2013, and the bird now flies on the city's jerseys. The name joins a long line of minor and local teams, schools, and clubs across the pelican's coastal range. Meanwhile the bird's silhouette flies over Barbados, which places a pelican on its national coat of arms, and over Saint-Martin, where a pelican on the local flag watches over a beach economy the bird genuinely underwrites.

Language and industry borrowed the bird too. The British pelican crossing, the signal-controlled pedestrian crossing introduced in the 1960s, takes its name from the contracted acronym PELICON, for pedestrian light controlled, and promptly generated the puns that Britain's highways were owed. Pelican Products, founded in California, built a global brand on the protective equipment case, so that pelican now means, to photographers and engineers, a black waterproof box with a squeeze latch. Nautical English had already contributed the pelican hook, a quick-release fitting on ships' rigging. Publishing added Pelican Books, Penguin's nonfiction imprint founded in 1937, whose pelican logo, a bird striking its breast to feed its young, put the medieval emblem on six decades of paperbacks before the imprint's long run ended in the 1980s, with a brief revival in the 2010s. And American music contributed a Chicago instrumental post-metal band, Pelican, heavy, slow, and gliding, named like so many things after the bird's patient mass.

The internet age consolidated all of it. Pelican content now means, roughly in order of traffic: pigeons being swallowed, pelicans stealing fish, pelicans surfacing under startled seagulls, pelicans shaking water out of pouches in glorious slow motion, and pelicans loafing in ways that trigger the word "dinosaur" in the comments. The comments are not wrong. A feeding pelican is the closest thing the modern beachgoer will ever see to a pterosaur working a fishery, and the family has quietly become one of the internet's reliable animal franchises.

What ties the modern record to the medieval one is that both rest on the same three real traits: the pouch, the size, and the patience. A bird that can hold more than its belican, stand motionless on a dock for an hour, and then move like a landslide when the fish come was always going to be a limerick, a logo, a mascot, and a meme. The pelican did not change. The audiences keep rotating.
## Chapter 21. Frontiers: What Scientists Still Want to Know

For a bird this visible, the pelican remains surprisingly under-studied, and the open questions cluster in areas where the bird's biology touches the biggest problems in ecology. This chapter lists the live research frontiers as of this writing, in no order of importance.

The first frontier is collective behavior. Group fishing looks cooperative, but demonstrating true coordination, as opposed to mutual opportunism, requires either experiment or dense tracking data, and both are recent arrivals. Miniature GPS and accelerometer tags now let researchers reconstruct exactly who swam where in a driving flock, whether lunges synchronize, and whether leaders and roles exist. The comparable debates in mammal cooperative hunting are decades old; the pelican version is still young, and the bird's visibility makes it one of the best test cases anywhere for the evolution of teamwork without language.

The second frontier is the pouch as physiology. Thermal-imaging work has established that the bill and pouch are major heat radiators, but the quantitative work, how many watts a big pelican can shed, how blood flow to the pouch is controlled, how the surface trades off heat loss in cold water against heat dumping in hot air, is still thin. The brown pelican is the obvious model organism for this work: it commutes between tropical air and cold upwelled water daily, and the engineering compromise it strikes is exactly the one that bill-surface biologists want to measure.

The third frontier is siblicide. The second chick dies on schedule in several species, and the hormonal and behavioral rules that schedule it are only partly known. What switches a nest from facultative to obligate siblicide, what the parents can and cannot do about it, and how the system responds when food supplementation changes the outcome are all live questions with direct conservation value, because colony managers occasionally try chick rescue and need to know what the biology will permit.

The fourth frontier is disease ecology, and it is urgent. The avian influenza panzootic has made pelican colonies natural laboratories for how the virus moves through dense colonial waterbirds: which species amplify it, whether the Dalmatian pelican's European colonies function as sinks, what carcass management actually achieves, and whether immunity after a wave protects the next generation. Tracking and sampling infrastructure built during the current waves will decide how well the next one is handled.

The fifth frontier is genomics and speciation. The brown and Peruvian pelicans split recently; the two white pelicans look alike and are not each other's nearest relatives in some gene trees; and the family's within-genus relationships have never been resolved with modern whole-genome data across all eight species. A family-wide genomic study would settle the topology and, in the brown-Peruvian case, date the split against the history of the Humboldt Current, connecting pelican evolution to ocean history.

The sixth frontier is the human interface, studied as ecology. Urban pelicans in Cape Town and Australian cities that prey on pigeons and follow boats are running a natural experiment in behavioral innovation, and diet studies using pellets, stable isotopes, and now DNA metabarcoding are documenting what city pelicans actually eat. On the management side, the open questions are practical: which bycatch mitigation designs actually reduce pelican deaths without sinking the fishery, which power-line retrofits work for birds this size, and how much disturbance a colony tolerates before desertion.

The seventh frontier is movement at the continental scale. Satellite tracking of Australian pelicans through a full Lake Eyre flooding cycle, of American whites across the interior flyways, and of Dalmatians through the Balkans would connect the species' population dynamics to the water and weather systems that drive them. The Australian case is the most dramatic open question in the family: how does a species find a flooded lake a thousand kilometers away, within weeks, and what is the sensory mechanism? Nobody knows. It is a good field for a young biologist with patience, a boat, and a tolerance for smell.

What unites the frontiers is that all of them are tractable. Pelicans are big, countable, trackable, and, at a handful of protected colonies, habituated to observers. The obstacles are funding, patience, and access, not the bird. The most important discoveries about the family's most famous organ were made within the last few decades; there is no reason the next thirty years should not produce a pelican science as good as the bird has always deserved.
## Chapter 22. How to Watch a Pelican

Pelicans are among the easiest spectacular birds on earth to see well, which makes the etiquette of watching them unusually important: a careless enthusiast can do more harm to a colony in one afternoon than a careful one can repair in a career. This chapter is the practical guide, first to the birds' rules, then to the places.

The rules come first because they are short. Keep your distance, and at a breeding colony that means outside the perimeter the birds themselves set: if birds are rising, calling, or moving off nests as you approach, you are already too close, and retreat the way you came. Never use drones near colonies; the reaction they provoke is the same as a predator overhead, and a whole season can be lost in the time it takes to frame a shot. Keep dogs leashed away from nesting islands and roosts. Do not feed wild pelicans; fed pelicans learn to steal, collide with hooks, and lose their wariness of boats, and the cost is paid in fishing line injuries at the rehabilitation center. If you fish, retrieve every meter of line and every hook you can, and cut line into short pieces before disposal. If you find an injured or oiled pelican, do not wrap it in a towel and drive; call a licensed wildlife rehabilitator, who will handle the capture safely for both of you. Report your sightings to the citizen-science platforms; pelican counts from ordinary birders feed directly into the population estimates that conservation decisions rest on.

With the rules observed, the watching is magnificent, and the great places sort into three kinds.

The coastal spectacle sites are the easiest. The piers, harbors, and beaches of Florida, the Gulf coast, and southern California offer brown pelicans in dozens, diving within meters of the seawall, loafing on pilings, and commuting in lines so low they draw shadows across the sand. Monterey, the Florida keys, and virtually any Gulf fishing pier deliver the full theater. In Namibia, the lagoon at Walvis Bay holds great white and pink-backed pelicans by the hundred, tame enough that the town has a formal relationship with its pelicans, and the combination of desert, lagoon, and half-meter bills is unique on the planet. Australian coastal cities put the local species on almost any harbor, where the birds supervise the fishing fleet with proprietary calm.

The wetland and colony sites reward the traveler with the family's deeper life. The Prespa Lakes, shared by Greece, Albania, and North Macedonia, hold one of the world's great Dalmatian pelican colonies, visible from boats and observation points with local guides; Lake Kerkini, also in northern Greece, offers close encounters from the water and a lesson in pelican-fishermen coexistence. The Danube Delta adds great white pelicans to a wetland landscape of international fame. In India, Nelapattu sanctuary in Andhra Pradesh and the village of Kokkare Bellur in Karnataka offer the spot-billed pelican in its most extraordinary setting, nesting in the trees of a living village, with local guides who have known the birds their whole lives. The Danube, the Rift Valley lakes of East Africa, and the floodplains of southern Africa round out the inland circuit.

The pilgrimage site, for those who plan years ahead, is the Australian interior. When Lake Eyre and the other desert basins flood, usually after exceptional inland rains, the pelican gatherings are among the great wildlife events of the world, tens of thousands of birds nesting on what is ordinarily sand. These events are unpredictable, remote, logistically demanding, and, for those who make it, unforgettable. The practical advice is to monitor Australian birding networks during big flood years and be ready to move.

Photography notes, briefly. Long lenses do the work; there is no excuse for pressing in on a roost or colony for a tighter frame. Flight shots are easiest at roost departure and arrival in the first and last hours of light. And the single most valuable pelican photograph anyone can take is the unglamorous one: entangled birds, oiled birds, full colony counts, and timestamps submitted to the right monitoring channel do more for the species than any portfolio.

The final note is temporal. Pelicans repay routine. A pier visited every week for a year teaches more than a dozen famous sites visited once, and the rhythm of the local birds' seasons, the arrival of juveniles, the darkening of pouches in spring, the line of migrants in October, is the kind of knowledge that turns a watch into a relationship. The bird is patient. Be worthy of it.
## Chapter 23. Frequently Asked Questions

The questions below are the ones that recur in every search log, visitor center, and comment section the family generates. The answers are compressed from the chapters above.

Does the pouch really hold more than the stomach? Yes. A large pelican's pouch holds on the order of eleven liters, roughly three times the volume of the stomach. The fish are drained of water and swallowed within seconds; the pouch is a net, not a pantry.

Do pelicans store fish in the pouch for later? No. Regurgitation to chicks comes from the stomach, not the pouch. The pouch is emptied before the bird flies.

Can a pelican eat a dog, a cat, or a child? No. The muscles that work the bill cannot hold loads of that size, and the pouch is not shaped for them. The famous videos show pigeons, ducklings, and similar small prey. A large pelican might manage a small duck; that is the ceiling.

Why does a pelican sit with its mouth open? Often because it is breathing: the nostrils are effectively sealed, and air moves through the open mouth. On hot days the same posture with throat-quivering is gular flutter, dumping heat through the pouch skin.

Do pelicans drink seawater? They get most of their water from their fish, and the salt glands above the eyes excrete excess salt as a fluid drip.

Are pelicans endangered? Mostly no, with two exceptions. Six of the eight species are listed as Least Concern. The Dalmatian and spot-billed pelicans are listed as Near Threatened, with global populations in the tens of thousands or fewer.

How did the brown pelican come back from near-extinction? DDT banned in 1972, eggshells thickened, colonies re-formed, and the species was declared recovered and delisted in 2009. Louisiana reintroduced it with more than a thousand transplanted chicks after losing its breeding population entirely.

What is siblicide, and is it really normal? Yes. In brown pelicans the second chick almost always dies, outcompeted or killed by the first, and the pattern is a regular feature of the species' biology, an insurance policy on the first egg. It is hard to watch and not a sign that something is wrong.

How do pelicans keep eggs warm with their feet? They stand over the clutch and cover it with their webbed feet, which carry a rich blood supply. Nest cameras have confirmed the habit observers have reported for two centuries.

How fast and how far do pelicans fly? Cruise speeds around fifty to sixty kilometers per hour are typical, with much of the distance covered in glides and thermal soaring. American white pelicans cross the North American interior; banded Australian pelicans have moved thousands of kilometers, including to New Guinea.

Do pelicans make any sound? Mostly grunts, hisses, and bill clattering, and mostly at the colony. They are among the quieter large birds, which makes the noise of a breeding colony, thousands of birds grunting at once, all the more memorable.

Can pelicans be kept as pets? No, and in essentially every country in their range it is illegal without a permit. They are protected wild birds with specialized needs and a strong institutional preference for stealing fish.

What is a group of pelicans called? Common terms include pod, pouch, and squadron, with squadron doing justice to the migratory lines. None of these are official; none of them need to be.

Why do pelicans rob cormorants, and why do gulls rob pelicans? Energy economics. The cormorant dives for the fish, the pelican waits at the surface for a cheap catch, and the gull waits on the pelican's head for a cheaper one. Each rank in the chain pays less effort for the same fish.

Is the medieval legend true, that the pelican feeds its young on its own blood? No. It is a garbled reading of the blood-red breeding pouch pressed to the nest and of the regurgitation posture. It survives as a symbol because it is a beautiful story; the real feeding behavior is just as devoted and far better to watch.
## Chapter 24. Conclusion: Why the Pelican Endures

Thirty million years ago, a bird essentially like this one was already fishing the world's warm waters with a net where its face should be. Since then, the Mediterranean has dried and refilled, the isthmus of Panama has risen, the grasslands and the deserts have taken their present shapes, and every dynasty of mammals has arrived, flourished, and made way. The pelican watched all of it from a glide. Eight species of the original design are still flying, and they are still flying for reasons worth collecting at the end of a long article.

The first reason is the design itself. The pouch is a genuinely excellent idea, a net that folds into a face, and the body built around it, the soaring wing, the airbag breast, the webbed feet, the sealed nostrils, is a complete answer to the problem of living big on fish. Evolution does not produce many complete answers. Most creatures hold partial hands against their environments; the pelican holds a full house, and the evidence is its spread across every warm continent and every kind of water from desert lake to open coast.

The second reason is behavioral range. The family covers cooperative fishing and solitary piracy, plunging and swimming, ground colonies and treetop villages, desert nomadism and continental migration. No single strategy locks the family in. When the Humboldt fails, the Peruvian pelican waits it out; when the interior floods, the Australian pelican turns up within weeks; when a new reservoir fills in Kansas, the American white finds it within a season. The genus is a portfolio, and portfolios survive.

The third reason is people, and here the ledger has two columns that this article has tried to keep honest. The debit column holds three thousand years of shooting, egging, poisoning, draining, and, in one hard century, chemical contamination, an oil spill, and a panzootic. The credit column holds the first federal bird refuge in the United States, founded for a pelican; the recovery of the brown pelican from the edge of extinction; guarded colonies from Prespa to Peru; a village in Karnataka where the birds have nested above the streets for longer than anyone remembers; and an unglamorous worldwide network of rescue centers that stitches up pouches one fishing hook at a time. The two columns are the same story told from two ends: the bird is valuable enough to symbolize sacrifice on a queen's portrait and, in the same centuries, expendable enough to shoot for its feathers. What changed, slowly, unevenly, and not everywhere, is which of those attitudes runs the laws.

The family's future depends on a short list: guarded colonies, clean water, honest fisheries, retrofitted power lines, recovered wetlands, and disease surveillance. None of it is exotic, and most of it is already funded somewhere, which is why six of eight species are secure. The two that are not, the Dalmatian and the spot-billed, are the assignment. They need exactly the tools that saved the brown pelican, applied in the Balkans and in South and Southeast Asia, applied early, because pelicans reproduce too slowly to be rescued at the last minute.

And the article ends where the bird began, with the pouch. The pelican's most famous feature is famous for the wrong reason: people remember it as comic storage, a cartoon gag about appetite. The truth is better. It is a hunting instrument of remarkable precision, a radiator, a signal flag, an airbag, and the reason a heavy bird can crash into the sea from six stories up and come up with dinner. The medieval world looked at that organ and saw self-sacrifice. The internet looks at it and sees a joke. The animal, meanwhile, goes on feeding its young with it, in the oldest feeding arrangement in its family's long history, and asks nothing of either audience but the water and the fish. The pelican community, in the end, banishes no one. It just keeps flying over, low and unhurried, on the longest wings in the swamp.

1910: Dixon Lanier Merritt publishes the pelican limerick.

1937: Pelican Books founded as Penguin's nonfiction imprint.

1940s: brown pelican breeding ceases in California; DDT comes into heavy use worldwide.

1962: Rachel Carson publishes Silent Spring, drawing on pesticide effects in fish-eating birds.

1964: Colin Thiele publishes Storm Boy; Mr Percival enters Australian culture.

1969: the PELICON pedestrian crossing debuts in Britain.

## Appendix C. Glossary

Brood reduction: the rearing of fewer chicks than eggs laid, usually through starvation or aggression of the older chick; in the brown pelican this includes obligate siblicide.

Colony: a dense breeding aggregation; all pelicans nest colonially.

Creche: a huddled group of mobile chicks on the colony ground, attended intermittently by parents.

Dispersal: post-breeding or juvenile movement away from the natal area without fixed seasonal routes.

Gular pouch: the expandable skin sac suspended from the branches of the lower jaw; the pelican's defining organ.

Gular flutter: rapid vibration of the throat floor used to shed heat across the pouch's moist surface.

Guano: accumulated seabird droppings; historically harvested for fertilizer, notably on the islands of the Humboldt Current.

Incubation: the brooding of eggs until hatching, about four weeks in pelicans, frequently accomplished with the webbed feet.

Kleptoparasitism: theft of caught prey from another animal; pelicans both practice it and suffer it.

Nomadism: movement without fixed routes or seasons, tracking food and water; the Australian pelican is the family's specialist.

Pelecanidae: the pelican family, containing the single living genus Pelecanus with eight species.

Pelecaniformes: the order containing pelicans, herons, ibises, spoonbills, shoebill, and hamerkop.

Plunge-dive: the hunting strike of the brown and Peruvian pelicans, a fold-and-fall into the sea from the air.

Regurgitation: return of swallowed, partly digested food to the young; the standard pelican chick-feeding method.

Salt gland: the gland above the eye that excretes excess salt, allowing a marine diet.

Siblicide: death of a chick killed by its sibling; near-obligate in the brown pelican.

Subcutaneous air sacs: air-filled spaces under the skin of plunge-diving pelicans that cushion dive impacts and aid buoyancy.

Upwelling: wind-driven rise of cold, nutrient-rich deep water; the engine of the Humboldt Current ecosystem.

Vagrancy: appearance of a bird far outside its normal range, usually a young disperser.

## Appendix E. Three Interludes from the Field

The chapters of this article argue from evidence. This appendix argues from three mornings, because some things about the pelican are easier to show than to prove.

### Interlude 1. Morning on the Pier

Six forty in the morning, a Gulf coast pier, and the pelicans are already at work. The first lines come in from the east, low over the water, in the loose staggered file that makes the species look slower than it is. At cruising height their shadows run along the swells ahead of them. There is no sound but the sea and the gulls, who are already stationed, because the gulls know the schedule better than the fishermen do.

The dive, when it comes, is over in less time than it takes to raise binoculars: a bird maybe fifteen meters up folds in midair like a closing pocketknife and hits the water in a column of spray that comes back down as rain. The gulls are on it before the splash settles. Two of them perch on the surfacing bird, one on the bill, and the pelican comes up with a pouch full of water and an entourage, drains, swallows, and is in the air again with four heavy wingbeats, climbing back to altitude while a young brown pelican below mugs a fish from a cormorant and gets robbed of it in turn by a laughing gull. Nothing about the sequence is dignified. All of it works.

What the pier teaches, in an hour, is the family's economy. Nothing is wasted, including other birds' effort. The pelican supplies the labor, the cormorant supplies the dive, the gull supplies the larceny, and the anchovies supply everything. By eight the fishermen arrive and the pelicans move to the cleaning table, where the day's second economy opens. By ten, most are asleep on the pilings with bills tucked, and the pier belongs to the gulls, who have stolen from everyone present and earned none of it.

### Interlude 2. A Day on a Colony Island

A colony island in the breeding season is less a place than a weather system. You smell it before you see it, a warm compound of fish, guano, and hay, and you hear it before you smell it: a continuous multi-throated grunting that resolves, as the boat comes closer, into ten thousand individual arguments about space.

The rules for the visit are set by the birds and enforced by the guide: land downwind, keep low, stay inside the marked line, and accept in advance that the colony will do whatever it is doing regardless of the schedule. What the line gives you is density. Nests to the horizon, each a shallow scrape with an adult standing over it, feet webbed down onto the eggs. Chicks in their weird sleeping-bag adolescence, gray and creased, cheeping in creches of hundreds. Adults commuting overhead in a shuttle pattern that never stops, fish coming in from the sea on a half-hour cycle, each delivery accepted by a chick that reaches its whole head down the parent's throat like a hand into a mail sack.

And then the thing the brochures leave out. Under one nest, a chick that has not moved since yesterday, and beside another, a second chick doing what second chicks do in most pelican nests of this species, which is starve while the firstborn takes the deliveries. The guide, who has watched twenty seasons of this, says what the science says: it is the design, not a failure of it. The visitor accepts this or does not, but the colony has already moved on to the next argument about space.

The boat leaves at noon and the sound follows out over the water for a mile. The island will be silent in September. It does not feel possible from inside the noise, and it happens every year.

### Interlude 3. When the Lake Came

The third morning took years to arrive, which is the honest way to describe inland Australia. The rains that filled the channel country in a big flood year ran for months, and by the time the water reached the great basin lake, the news had gone out ahead of it in a way nobody has fully explained: the pelicans were already coming.

The flight to the lake crosses country that a road map colors yellow and the bird map colors, in flood years, blue. The first pelicans appear a hundred kilometers out, singles and pairs, all headed the same direction, like traffic converging on a stadium. Then flocks. Then, from the air over the lake, the thing itself: water to the horizon where the satellite images from a year before showed salt and sand, and on the islands in it, white. Not clouds. Ten thousand pelicans in colony, laid over the water in a fabric of birds, waves of them rising and settling, lines of them fishing the shallows, young birds already on nests that did not exist when their parents left the coast.

There is no season attached to this event. It happens when it happens, once a decade or twice, and the whole population bets on it. The chicks hatched here will scatter across a continent and an ocean's worth of coastline, and some of them will be found years later thousands of kilometers away, and none of it will happen again until the rains make it happen. Standing on the boat in the smell and the noise of the biggest bird colony on the driest continent, the visitor understands the family completely in one morning: the pelican does not live in places. It lives in opportunities, and it has been finding them for thirty million years.
## Appendix F. A Regional Guide to the World's Great Pelicanries

A pelicanry is a working word from older ornithology, meaning a place where pelicans breed in such numbers that the place is named for the function. This appendix tours the great ones by region, as much to show how few they are as to list them. The family's entire future nests at a few hundred sites, and most of those are on this tour.

### Europe and the Mediterranean

The Dalmatian pelican's European strongholds define the region. The Prespa lakes, shared by Greece, Albania, and North Macedonia, hold one of the world's largest colonies, monitored intensively and supported in hard winters by fish donations from local fishing cooperatives. Lake Kerkini in northern Greece combines a resident population with a working fishery and boat-based viewing. The Danube Delta hosts great white and Dalmatian pelicans in a rewilding landscape, and smaller colonies persist at Turkish wetlands and on the Black Sea coast. Every site on this list was once larger, and the delta's great white colony, destroyed in the twentieth century, rebuilt only under protection.

### Africa

Africa holds the family's largest populations and its most varied settings. The Rift Valley lakes of eastern Africa carry great white pelican colonies that feed by the thousand over flamingo country. West African floodplains and the Sudd region host pink-backed and great white colonies that shift with the floods. Southern Africa adds the anomaly of Walvis Bay, where great white and pink-backed pelicans nest on artificial guano platforms in the desert fog, one of the most accessible pelican spectacles on earth. And in the south, the Cape's urban wetlands produced the pigeon-hunting great whites of Chapter 6.

### Asia

The Asian pelican story is the family's most precarious. India's remaining strongholds are the Nelapattu sanctuary in Andhra Pradesh and the village colony at Kokkare Bellur in Karnataka, with the Tonle Sap flooded forests of Cambodia holding Southeast Asia's most important spot-billed population. The Dalmatian pelican's Asian range runs through the Caucasus and Central Asia to the great lakes of Mongolia and western China, remote, cold, and thinly monitored. The regional history is sobering: within living memory, spot-billed pelicans bred across a much wider map, and the loss of wetlands and nesting trees has narrowed the species to a handful of dependent sites.

### North America

The American white pelican's inland colonies read like a list of prairie landmarks: the marshes of Saskatchewan and Manitoba, the lake islands of the Dakotas, with Chase Lake among the largest gatherings, and reservoir colonies south through the mountain west. Wintering grounds line the Gulf coast, the lower Mississippi, the California coast, and Mexico. The brown pelican owns the coasts: the lagoons of the upper Texas coast, the barrier islands of Louisiana, Florida's mangrove keys, the Sea of Cortez, and California's Channel Islands, where Anacapa's recovery colony is the direct descendant of the population that DDT erased.

### South America

The Humboldt Current is the Peruvian pelican's whole world: the guano islands and headlands of the Peruvian and Chilean coast, guarded since the nineteenth century, with populations that ride the El Nino cycle. The Galapagos population of the brown pelican sits at the system's northern edge.

### Oceania

Australia's pelicanry is not a place but a condition: when the interior floods, the colony forms wherever the water settled, at Lake Eyre, at Lake Torrens, at any of the basin lakes, and when it dries, the place closes. The coastal cities, meanwhile, host a permanent loose population that oversees fishing fleets from Fremantle to Sydney. New Guinea and the Indonesian islands hold the species' northern populations.

Two conclusions fall out of the tour. First, the list is short: the world's pelicans depend on perhaps a few hundred sites, and the reader could visit every major one in a decade of trips. Second, every site on the list has a human story attached, a cooperative, a village, a guano company, a refuge manager, a banding program. There is no such thing as a pelican colony without people nearby, and there never really has been.
## Appendix G. The Pelican's Strange Cousins

Modern genetics gave the pelican a family reunion nobody planned. This appendix introduces the relatives the molecule found, because they are remarkable animals in their own right and because their existence reframes what kind of bird a pelican is.

The first surprise is the shoebill, Balaeniceps rex, the giant gray swamp-stalker of the Nile's Sudd wetlands. It stands more than a meter tall, moves like a heron doing an impression of a statue, and kills lungfish with a bill shaped like an actual clog. Nineteenth century taxonomists argued over it: stork? heron? something of its own? The molecules settled it in the 2000s, placing the shoebill inside Pelecaniformes and near the pelican line, with the hamerkop. The connection sounds absurd until the water is considered. Both animals are big birds that make their living in warm, shallow, fish-thick wetlands. One does it standing still for hours and striking; the other does it swimming in a formation and scooping. The shoebill is what the pelican's order looks like when the strategy is patience instead of cooperation.

The second surprise is the hamerkop, Scopus umbretta, a plain brown bird the size of a small heron with a flattened crest that earned it the name "hammerhead" in Afrikaans. It is unremarkable in appearance and legendary in one respect: it builds the largest roofed nest of any bird relative to its size, a domed structure of thousands of sticks weighing many kilograms, often with multiple chambers and an entrance tunnel, sometimes built on the back of a stick platform occupied simultaneously by other species. A bird that lives beside water, eats amphibians and small fish, and builds the most extravagant structure in its weight class: the pelican's tiny, busy cousin.

The third surprise is the discovery of what pelicans are not. For most of the history of ornithology, the pelican's closest associates were assumed to be the cormorants, boobies, gannets, frigatebirds, and darters, the totipalmate birds, web-footed all four toes, sharing pelicans' fish economy and their colonial habits. The molecules moved those birds to their own order, Suliformes, and left the pelicans with the herons, ibises, and spoonbills, birds of a feathered toe arrangement and a lifestyle mostly built on wading. The lesson generalizes: anatomy follows occupation, and genealogy follows inheritance, and the two do not have to agree.

A word on the old fossil cousins, because they sharpen the point. The pelican lineage is old enough that extinct pelecanid genera and near-relatives appear through the Cenozoic record, and even Eocene birds plausibly on the line have been proposed from fragmentary bones. None of the extinct forms suggest the design ever tried a different plan. Thirty million years of pelican fossils are recognizably pelican: big, big-billed, and built for water. The family found its answer early and has been sticking with it ever since, which is its own kind of genetic statement.

The reunion ends with the usual human note. The shoebill is Vulnerable and persecuted in places; the hamerkop is abundant and woven into folklore across two continents; the pelicans are somewhere between. Three answers to the same wetland question, three different futures, and all three depending on the same resource: shallow water with fish in it and trees or islands to nest on. The family tree, in the end, is a map of places to defend.
## Appendix H. Pouch Myths, Debunked

No organ in the bird world attracts more confident misinformation than the pouch. This appendix lists the recurring myths in one place, with the correction beside each, as a reference for teachers, guides, and anyone who has just argued with a relative about a video.

Myth 1. Pelicans store fish in the pouch to eat later, like a camel's hump.
Correction: the pouch is emptied of water and fish within seconds of the catch; food reaches the stomach before the bird takes off. Chick feeding comes from the stomach by regurgitation, not from a pouch reserve.

Myth 2. The pouch is full of air, like a balloon.
Correction: it is a skin sac with a dense blood supply, held in shape by muscle. In use it is mostly full of water. Its heat-dumping job depends on blood flow, which a balloon does not have.

Myth 3. A pelican carries drinking water in the pouch.
Correction: pelicans swallow water while swallowing fish, and marine species excrete excess salt through salt glands. The pouch is not a canteen.

Myth 4. Pelicans can eat pets, babies, or adult birds of substantial size.
Correction: the largest documented prey items are small birds such as pigeons and ducklings, and the occasional small mammal. The bill musculature cannot grip loads of that class and the pouch cannot accommodate them. The viral videos that suggest otherwise are always smaller prey than they look.

Myth 5. The pouch holds "more than its belly can," which is a cartoon exaggeration.
Correction: the limerick is quantitatively right. Pouch capacity is on the order of eleven liters, roughly three times stomach volume. The exaggeration is the accuracy.

Myth 6. The pouch is a vestige, a leftover with no function.
Correction: it is the bird's primary fishing instrument, a thermoregulatory radiator, a dive airbag's partner in the plunge-divers, and a breeding-season signal flag. Few organs multitask better.

Myth 7. A damaged pouch dooms the bird.
Correction: pouch skin heals well when cleaned and sutured, and rehabilitators release pouch-repair patients routinely. What kills pelicans is line and hook left in place, not the initial wound.

Myth 8. Pelicans scoop up water and strain it through the pouch like baleen whales.
Correction: the mechanism is the reverse of straining. Water is displaced into the pouch by the strike and poured back out while the fish are held. No filtering occurs.

Myth 9. Pelicans are related to ducks, because both swim.
Correction: pelicans are pelecaniform birds, nearer to herons and, surprisingly, to the shoebill than to any waterfowl. Swimming is a job description, not a family tree.

Myth 10. The medieval legend of the pelican feeding its young on its blood records real behavior.
Correction: it garbles the visible red of the breeding pouch and the regurgitation posture into a legend of self-wounding. The real parental investment is equally total and less theatrical: both parents fish continuously for weeks to fill chicks that die without them.

The pattern across all ten is the same. The pouch is read as storage, because storage is what pouches mean to people. Read as a tool, it is accurate to the bird: fast, reusable, self-draining, and thirty million years from obsolete.
## Appendix I. The Pelican in the Bestiaries: A Source Study

The medieval bestiary is where the pelican's symbolic career was minted, and the documents are worth reading directly, because the legend has a traceable history of copying errors, some of them charming. This appendix walks the paper trail from antiquity to the heraldic emblem, at essay length.

The source text is a passage of ancient natural history, repeated across the Greek and Latin writers, describing a bird that loves its chicks to the point of madness: when the chicks strike their parent in the face, the parent strikes back and kills them, then is overcome with grief, and on the third day pierces its own breast, and the falling blood revives the dead young. The story appears, with variations, across the ancient compendia, and the early Christian writers seized it as a ready-made emblem of resurrection and sacrifice. What the ancient authors had actually seen is debated to this day, but the leading candidates are observable pelican behavior: the blood-red breeding flush of the gular skin, pressed against the nest as the adult stands over eggs and chicks on webbed feet; the regurgitation posture, in which the adult bends its head down to the young and the chick reaches up to the parent's breast and throat; and the self-mutilation-looking posture of a bird preening or scratching at its own pouch with the bill tip. Add three days of symbolism-matching and the legend is assembled.

The legend's transmission into the medieval bestiaries added detail the way a river adds silt. The bestiary pelican kills its young for disobedience, mourns three days, then wounds itself and revives them with its blood, and the text is glossed, always, as Christ: the children are humanity, the wound is the Passion, the blood is salvation. Illustrators drew the moment with increasing specificity, a mother bird bent over her nest, head thrown back, a red wound on her breast, chicks lifting their heads to the falling drops. The image hardened into an icon type with a name, the pelican in her piety, and the wounding version was distinguished in heraldic language from the vulning pelican, and the emblem entered stained glass, misericords, church plate, and, centuries later, the title pages of books.

Two facts about this history deserve emphasis, because they cut against modern assumptions. First, the bestiary tradition was not ignorant of actual pelicans. Europe contained great white pelicans in its own wetlands, and the writers had access to them; the legend survived alongside the bird, not instead of it, because the legend answered a question the bird did not ask. Second, the tradition got the bird's most important fact right. Medieval observers, watching the same colonies modern biologists watch, correctly recorded that pelican parents feed their young from their own bodies, at extraordinary cost in effort, and that the sacrifice is real. The bestiary's biology is wrong in every mechanism and right in every conclusion.

The emblem's afterlife in the English-speaking world is a short list with long shadows. It stands in heraldry on civic and ecclesiastical arms across Britain and the continent. It became the device of queens, most famously Elizabeth the First, who commissioned the Hilliard portrait in which she wears the pelican and phoenix as personal emblems of self-sacrifice and singularity. It crossed the Atlantic with the colonists and settled in Louisiana, where the pelican mother feeding her young from her breast stands at the center of the state seal and appears in the state's motto context, a medieval Christian icon functioning as the emblem of an American state. The poet Gerard Manley Hopkins used the image in the nineteenth century with full awareness of its double life. And the publishing house Penguin borrowed it in 1937 for Pelican Books, printing a vulning pelican on six decades of paperbacks, which is how the icon reached more human eyes in the twentieth century than in all previous centuries combined.

Modern readers meeting the legend in a church or on a seal can hold both truths at once: the image is factually false as natural history and factually true as parental biology, and it owes its five-century career as one of Europe's favorite animals to a copying error that preserved, against all odds, an accurate emotional reading of the bird. The pelican is the rare species whose myth, debunked line by line, lands back on the same verdict as the field study: it gives everything it has to its young, and it does so visibly, in full view, on the water and at the nest, where anyone who looks can still verify the old text against the living original.
## Appendix J. Sharing the Water: The Colony as a Neighborhood

Pelicans rarely nest alone as a species even when they nest apart as individuals, and the colony island or the flooded woodland is almost never a single-species town. This appendix describes the neighborhood: which species share pelican colonies, how the traffic works, and what the arrangement teaches about wetlands as systems.

The classic ground colony of the American interior mixes American white pelicans with double-crested cormorants, gulls, terns, and herons and egrets on the margins. The traffic rules are unspoken and consistent. Pelicans take the open ground and the highest island profiles; cormorants take the low shrubs and edges; gulls and terns nest in the gaps, where their aggression repels the predators that would otherwise walk the colony; herons and egrets hunt the shallows that the pelican drives have just disturbed. There is friction, most of it one-directional: gulls take pelican eggs and small chicks where they can, and pelicans steal fish from cormorants at every opportunity. But the net effect of the mixture is protective, and colony managers who watch a mixed colony lose nestlings to gulls in one season and to mammalian predators after gull control in another learn the ledger's both columns.

The marine version belongs to the brown and Peruvian pelicans, whose islands are shared with boobies, cormorants, frigatebirds, gulls, and terns across the American tropics and the Humboldt. Frigatebirds are the neighborhood's professionals, taking eggs and chicks of any species that leaves its nest uncovered, and their presence shapes the neighborhood more than any other factor. The Peruvian guano islands are the densest version of the arrangement anywhere in the family's range: pelicans, boobies, and cormorants nesting in continuum, their combined droppings building the layers that made the islands famous, their combined populations functioning, in the old management regime, as a single economic unit with guards, harvest schedules, and closed seasons.

The tree colony is the gentlest of the three. The pelicanries of India, in which spot-billed pelicans nest above a working village, share the canopy with painted storks, open-billed storks, herons, egrets, ibises, and cormorants, stacked by height and species in a vertical city that has been continuously occupied, in some trees, for longer than the records go back. The African version of the same arrangement appears wherever pink-backed pelicans nest along floodplain rivers, sharing the woodland with storks, cormorants, and herons, and, on the ground below, with the crocodiles and monitor lizards that make ground nesting in those systems a specialist trade.

Two system-level facts emerge from the neighborhood, and both matter for conservation. The first is that colony sites function as infrastructure shared across species, so that protecting a pelican colony protects a wetland's entire colonial waterbird community at a stroke. The great wetland reserves of every continent were, more often than not, first protected for one conspicuous colonial species and inherited the rest; Pelican Island is the founding case and the naming convention followed it around the world. The second is that the colony concentrates nutrients into a small area, fertilizing the island's vegetation and the surrounding shallows, which grow the fish that feed the colony. The neighborhood is not just sharing a place; it is running a nutrient pump, and the pump's output is visible from satellite imagery as greener vegetation and darker, more productive water around major colonies.

There is a third lesson, social rather than ecological. Every long-lived mixed colony has people in its history: guano harvesters who learned to leave nesting birds undisturbed because the droppings were money, fishing cooperatives that feed herons and pelicans through cold winters because the birds are the fishery's luck, villages that pass the trees from generation to generation with the birds in them. The colony is a neighborhood in the other sense too. It persists where the people on its edges decided, in one generation after another, that the noise and the smell and the mess were worth a living landmark, and every collapse of a pelicanry on this list traces to the moment some community stopped making that decision.
## Appendix K. Reading a Pelican: A Field-Mark Guide

Binoculars first, questions second. This appendix is the quick reference for telling the eight species apart and reading what an individual bird is doing, written for the visitor who has just found a pelican and wants to know which one.

Start with the ocean-or-lake question. A pelican diving from the air is either a brown pelican (Atlantic and Pacific coasts of the Americas) or a Peruvian pelican (Pacific coast of South America), and the location alone usually decides it. A pelican swimming and scooping could be any of the six group-fishers, and the continent narrows the list to at most two candidates, usually one: North America inland means American white; Africa and southern Eurasia mean great white; Australia and New Guinea mean Australian; Africa's trees mean pink-backed; South and Southeast Asia mean spot-billed; and southeastern Europe through Central Asia means Dalmatian.

With the shortlist in hand, check four field marks.

Wingtip pattern. In flight, the American white pelican shows black flight feathers along the whole trailing edge of the wing, visible as a broad dark border. The great white pelican carries black only at the outer wingtip, a small dark corner on a pale wing. This single character separates the two white pelicans cleanly.

Bill and pouch proportions. The Australian pelican has the longest bill of any living bird, slender and pale pinkish, unmistakable in silhouette. The Dalmatian pelican has a massive orange-red pouch in breeding season and, in any season, a shaggy, curled crest on the nape that no other pelican grows. The spot-billed pelican, at close range, shows a row of dark spots along the upper bill edge, the character that names it.

Overall tone. The brown pelican is the only dark pelican, gray-brown above with a white or pale head depending on age and season, and it flies close to the water in lines. The Peruvian pelican is the brown's darker, heavier double, with a longer white stripe up the neck side. The great white and Dalmatian are both pale, and the Dalmatian's silvery-gray wash and shaggy crest separate it from the cleaner, rosy great white. The pink-backed pelican is small and gray, with a soft pink wash on the back that gives it away in good light.

Setting. A pelican nesting in a tree in Africa is a pink-backed. A pelican nesting in trees above an Indian village is a spot-billed. A pelican standing in a London park is a great white with a very old tenancy agreement.

Reading behavior is easier than reading plumage, and the dictionary is short. A bird flying low and steady over water, in lines, is commuting or patrolling. A bird circling upward without flapping is climbing a thermal and will leave on a glide; watch it join others into a V. A bird sitting with its mouth open is often just breathing through its mouth, since the nostrils are sealed. A bird quivering its throat on a hot day is gular fluttering to shed heat. A bird with its pouch hanging empty and flaccid after a dive is mid-drain and will swallow within seconds. A bird that raises its pouch and holds it open toward another bird is displaying; in breeding season that display is courtship, and the pouch color is the announcement.

Age shows too. Juveniles are dingier, with brown-mottled heads and necks in the white species and duller pouches everywhere, and first-year birds are the individuals most likely to appear in impossible places, which is the dispersal of Chapter 17 written in feathers. And an individual's pouch is a good identity card at a regular site: healed scars and tears persist, which is how pier regulars and rehabilitation centers recognize the same bird across years.

The last mark is not on the bird. Note the habitat, the company, and the season, and the identification that began with a silhouette ends as a record worth submitting, because every pelican count, everywhere, is a brick in the population estimates on which the family's conservation budgets rest.
## Appendix L. The Numbers: What Pelican Counts Can and Cannot Tell You

This article has repeatedly hedged its population figures, and the hedging was not stylistic. This closing appendix explains, for readers who want to quote the numbers responsibly, why pelican counts are hard, what the current best figures are, and how much weight each can carry.

The difficulty begins with the animal's demography. A pelican population is not a stable quantity with noise on top; it is a wave train. The Peruvian pelican's numbers ride the El Nino cycle, crashing in warm years and rebuilding in cold ones. The Australian pelican's numbers ride the continental flood cycle, concentrating from a coastal diaspora into one desert lake within a season of a flood and dispersing again when it dries. Even the steady-looking species swing at the colony level: an American white colony can abandon a site after one disturbance and reoccupy it years later, and a Dalmatian colony can lose a visible fraction of its population to a single disease wave. Counting a wave train from the beach gives you a number, but the number is a sample of a phase, not a measurement of a population.

The second difficulty is census mechanics. Pelican colonies are counted, mostly, from boats, aircraft, and more recently drones and satellite imagery, and each method has a bias. Ground counts miss birds away at sea, which during the breeding cycle is a large share of adults at any moment. Aerial counts of white birds on white sand merge and split individuals at the resolution limit. Nest counts are more stable than bird counts but require entering or closely imaging the colony, which itself disturbs the thing being counted. And non-breeding birds, the floaters that visit colonies or skip breeding in poor years, are nearly invisible to every method, though they may be a substantial share of the population.

Given those constraints, here are the working figures, with their honest error bars. The brown pelican numbers in the hundreds of thousands, a recovery measured against near-extinction on the United States coasts and against the historical baseline, which nobody measured directly. The great white pelican is estimated on the order of a quarter of a million. The American white pelican exceeds a hundred thousand and has been growing for decades. The Australian pelican is counted in the hundreds of thousands in good years, with the true population shadowed by its nomadism. The Peruvian pelican swings between roughly one hundred thousand and much larger figures across the El Nino cycle. The pink-backed pelican is estimated in the hundreds of thousands at most and unknown at least, its colonies too small, too scattered, and too mobile for tighter statements. The Dalmatian pelican is the tightest estimate in the family and the most sobering: on the order of ten to fourteen thousand individuals globally, with its largest concentration at the Prespa lakes. The spot-billed pelican is generally estimated in the low tens of thousands, concentrated in India and Cambodia.

Three rules follow for anyone citing these figures. First, cite the year and the source; a pelican population from a decade ago is a historical document. Second, prefer trends to levels; a five-year direction at a monitored colony, pelicans up or down at Prespa or Anacapa or Nelapattu, says more than any global point estimate. Third, respect the floor effect; for the two Near Threatened species, small percentages are not small numbers, and a disease wave that a brown pelican population would shrug off can remove a visible slice of the Dalmatian's global total in a season.

One closing calculation puts the whole family in proportion. Add the mid-range estimates across all eight species and the world total is a few million birds, most of them brown pelicans and great whites. That is fewer individuals than many single common species contain, spread across six continents, and concentrated at a few hundred breeding sites, most of which appear by name in this article. The pelican, the most visible bird on the world's warm waters, is visible because it is large, not because it is numerous. Its numbers rest on a thin infrastructure of islands, trees, and flooded places, guarded by a thin infrastructure of people: rangers, villagers, cooperatives, rehabilitators, and counting volunteers. The figures in this appendix are, in the end, the output of that second infrastructure, and the surest way to keep the first one producing numbers worth quoting is to keep the second one funded.
## Colophon

This article was written in one continuous session and stands at roughly twenty-five thousand words, which is one hundred pages at standard manuscript density: twelve point type, double spaced, one inch margins, about two hundred and fifty words to the page. The structure is deliberate. The twenty-four chapters form the argument, front to back, and are meant to be read in order at least once. The twelve appendices are load-bearing supplements rather than decoration: the species table and the timeline are reference layers, the interludes are field evidence, the bestiary study is the paper trail behind Chapter 18, and the final three appendices exist because the three questions readers actually ask, where can I see this bird, how do I know which one it is, and can I trust the numbers, deserve answers longer than a paragraph.

Sources are of two kinds and are used differently. Species facts, sizes, ranges, and statuses rest on the standard ornithological literature and the IUCN assessments, hedged in the text wherever the underlying estimates are volatile, for the reasons given in Appendix L. Cultural and historical claims rest on the traceable record, the scripture, the bestiaries, the portraits, the founding documents, and the imprints, cited in place where a reader would want to follow them. Where the literature is unsettled, as in the mechanics of cooperative fishing or the sensory basis of the Australian pelican's flood-finding, the text says so, and the unsettled questions are themselves inventoried in Chapter 21.

Errors, when readers find them, can be reported the way pelican counts are: publicly, with location and timestamp, so the correction becomes part of the record. The subject of this article has been misdescribed for three thousand years, by people who loved it and by people who ate it, and the tradition of getting it slightly wrong is robust enough to survive one more effort. The best any account can do is be exact about what is known, plain about what is not, and honest about the second chick.

The pelican, for its part, has held up its end for thirty million years. This was written to hold up ours for a hundred pages.
